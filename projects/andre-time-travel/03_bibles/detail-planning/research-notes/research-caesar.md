# Julius Caesar, early 44 BCE: material-culture evidence for a no-face film shot (research notes)

Prepared 2026-10-09. Read/web research only. Nothing written to any repo.
Scope: Caesar seen only from behind / below the neck / hands / feet. Rome, Jan-Mar 44 BCE, before the Ides. Caesar was in his 56th year (Suet. Iul. 88.1: "He died in the fifty-sixth year of his age"), i.e. about 55.

Tags: DOC = documented about Caesar himself. RANK = documented for his rank/office (senator, dictator, pontifex maximus). GENERIC = period-generic (Roman aristocracy, not Caesar-specific). NONE = not found.
Source status: fetched-verified (page fetched; quote string-matched against the page text), search-snippet-only, could not verify. Source IDs (S01...) are defined in the table at the end.
Translation notes: Loeb translations (Rolfe, Cary, Perrin) as hosted on LacusCurtius; Smith's Dictionary is 1875 and dated, so it is used as a pointer to the ancient testimony it cites, not as final authority. Wikipedia is used only as a labelled secondary, never as the sole basis for a DOC tag.

## 1. Footwear

### 1.1 Caesar's own shoes: Cassius Dio 43.43.2 (DOC, single late source)
- S03, Dio 43.43.2, verbatim: "He used to show among all men his pride in rather loose clothing, and the footwear which he used later on was sometimes high and of a reddish colour, after the style of the kings who had once reigned in Alba, for he claimed that he was related to them through Iulus."
- Status: fetched-verified.
- Caveats:
  - The text says "footwear" (Greek hypodemata) that was "sometimes high and of a reddish colour". It does NOT say "boots", does not say leather, does not say laces, and gives no sole or fastening detail. "Red boots" is a gloss, not Dio's wording.
  - "Sometimes" means not always.
  - "Later on" places it after the decree in the same passage. 43.42 is the Parilia games after news of Munda (April 45), so the footwear belongs to 45-44 BCE. This dating is inferred from the narrative order, not stated by Dio.
  - Dio wrote about 250 years after the events. Treat as one literary source, plausible but not corroborated by Suetonius, Plutarch or Appian on this point.
  - Suetonius 45 (S01) says nothing about shoes. I searched the whole Suetonius Divus Iulius text for shoe/boot/footwear/buskin: no match. Plutarch Caesar (S04): no match.

### 1.2 Rank footwear for a senator: calceus senatorius (RANK)
- S08 Smith's Dictionary, "Calceus", verbatim: "Those who were elevated to the senate wore high shoes like buskins, fastened in front with four black thongs (nigris pellibus, Hor. Sat. I.6, 27) and adorned with a small crescent (Mart. II.29; Juv. VII.192)." Status: fetched-verified. Cites Horace (Augustan, a few years after 44), Martial and Juvenal (1st-2nd c. CE), so the details are later than 44 BCE.
- S08 also, verbatim: "Among the calcei worn by senators, those called mullei, from their resemblance to the scales of the red mullet (Isid. Or. XIX.34), were particularly admired".
- S18 Wikipedia "Calceus" (secondary), paraphrase marked: the "patrician calceus" seems often dyed red or purple; senators and higher priests were expected to wear the mulleus ("red calceus") with the toga praetexta in public duties; Festus claimed the mulleus was originally used by the kings of Alba Longa before being adopted by the patricians; the senatorial calceus had an ivory lunula attached. Wikipedia cites Festus 128L, Talbert 1984, Ryan 1998. Status: fetched-verified (page text), but secondary.
- Link between 1.1 and 1.2 (inference, mine, not stated by Dio): Dio's "reddish" footwear "after the style of the kings of Alba" lines up with the mulleus tradition that Festus attributes to the Alban kings. Dio does not use the word mulleus or calceus. Flag as inference.
- Whether Caesar, as patrician (gens Julia), held calceus patricius: RANK by inference; no source found that says it of Caesar by name. Tag: RANK only.
- The "high ... like buskins" description and the four black thongs are from Augustan-and-later sources (RANK for senators generally, GENERIC for 44 BCE exactness). I found no 1st-c. BCE archaeological example of a calceus senatorius. The Wikipedia page mentions a bronze senatorial calceus found in Spain (image caption, undated in the extract): could not verify date.

### 1.3 Indoors: soleae / slippers; shoes off at dinner (GENERIC, partly RANK)
- S09 Smith's Dictionary, "Solea", verbatim: "The solea, as worn by the upper classes, was adapted chiefly for wearing in the house, so that when a man went out to dinner, he walked in shoes [Calceus], taking with him slippers [Soccus] or soleae, which he put on when he entered the house. Before reclining at table, these were taken away by a servant". Cites Plautus Truculentus, Ovid Ars Amatoria II.212, Martial, Horace Sat. II.8.77. Status: fetched-verified.
- S08 "Calceus", verbatim: "The covering of the feet was removed before reclining at meals."
- S18 Wikipedia "Calceus" paraphrase: Martial says that by early imperial times hardly anyone wore calcei in leisure/rural settings; some high-ranking citizens in Rome preferred light Greek sandals or socci "to go with the crowd". Secondary.
- Tag: GENERIC (Roman upper class). Nothing found on what Caesar wore indoors at home: NONE.
- Production consequence (not a design decision): if feet are visible seated or reclining in a domestic scene, barefoot or soleae is supported for dining/reclining as GENERIC; outdoor/public-duty scenes support closed calceus as RANK. Anything specific to Caesar indoors is unsupported.

### 1.4 Barefoot floors and hearth warmth (GENERIC)
- S06 Vitruvius 7.4.5 (Gwilt trans.), verbatim: "the persons who wait on the guests, though barefooted, do not suffer from cold on this sort of pavement." Refers to the charcoal-and-lime "Grecian winter room" pavement. Shows barefoot attendants on a prepared floor; says nothing about guests or Caesar. Status: fetched-verified.

### 1.5 Sella curulis and the lower body (RANK, DOC for grants)
- Caesar was granted the curule chair in the Senate: S03 Dio 43.14.5: "They moreover voted that he should sit in the senate upon the curule chair with the successive consuls". DOC (decree), fetched-verified.
- Dio 44.4.2 (S05), verbatim: "they voted that he should always ride, even in the city itself, wearing the triumphal dress, and should sit in his chair of state everywhere except at the games". DOC (decree, early 44), fetched-verified.
- Gilded chair: Dio 44.6.1: "a gilded chair was granted him, and a garb that the kings had once used". Suet. Iul. 76.1: "a golden throne in the House and on the judgment seat". Plut. Caes. 61.4: "seated upon the rostra on a golden throne, arrayed in triumphal attire" (Lupercalia, 15 Feb 44). Dio 44.11.2: "sitting on the rostra in his gilded chair, adorned with the royal apparel and resplendent in his crown overlaid with gold". All DOC, fetched-verified. Note: the gilded/golden chair is a distinct object from the plain ivory-mounted sella curulis.
- Form of the sella curulis, S12 Smith "Sella": "In shape it long remained extremely plain, closely resembling a common folding (plicatilis) camp stool with crooked legs." Also: "The sella curulis appears from the first to have been ornamented with ivory". Also names two pairs of bronze legs preserved at Naples (Museo Borbonico). Status: fetched-verified. Right of use under the republic included "the dictator" (Smith cites Dio 43.48).
- S17 Wikipedia "Sella curulis" (secondary): "traditionally made of or veneered with ivory, with curved legs forming a wide X; it had no back, and low arms ... The chair could be folded". Caution: "low arms" is not supported by Smith (a camp stool); treat as unverified. Status: fetched-verified (page text), secondary.
- Why it matters for lower body: a backless folding X-frame stool shows the sitter's legs and calcei without any chair-back or side concealment; curved legs cross at ankle level. The toga hem and tunic hem fall between the sitter's knees and the crossed stool legs. This is GENERIC reasoning from the object form, not a documented image of Caesar seated.
- Public vs private: the curule chair/gilded chair are documented for the Senate, rostra, games and judgment seat (public). Nothing places it in a private house conversation. Domestic seating (sella, cathedra, scamnum, couch) for a private talk: NONE for Caesar.

## 2. Floor and room

### 2.1 Caesar's residence in 44 BCE (DOC for Domus Publica; flags the film-bible discrepancy)
- S01 Suet. Iul. 46.1, verbatim: "He lived at first in the Subura in a modest house, but after he became pontifex maximus, in the official residence on the Sacred Way." Status: fetched-verified. DOC. (Caesar became pontifex maximus in 63 BCE; the official residence is the domus publica.)
- S07 Platner & Ashby, "Regia" (LacusCurtius), verbatim: "The actual house of the pontifex was probably the domus publica, in the precinct of the Atrium Vestae ( Suet. Caes. 46 ; Cass. Dio LIV .24; ...), until Augustus transferred this residence to the Palatine". Status: fetched-verified. Note the word "probably" and that the primary sources do not use the Latin term "domus publica" in Suet. 46 (it says "in the official residence on the Sacred Way").
- S19 Wikipedia "House of the Vestals": "The domus publica, where the Pontifex Maximus dwelled, was located near the Atrium until that role was assumed by the emperors." Secondary, fetched-verified.
- Caesar's house had a gable ornament decreed by the Senate: Plut. Caes. 63.9 (S04) "there was attached to Caesar's house to give it adornment and distinction, by vote of the senate, a gable-ornament"; Suet. Iul. 81.3: Calpurnia "thought that the pediment of their house fell". DOC. This confirms a distinguishing exterior feature of the house in the last weeks, not any interior detail.
- Interior of the Domus Publica (floors, room layout, lighting): NONE. No excavation report was found that describes Caesar's rooms; the site is described only by location.
- DISCREPANCY (honest flag, no ruling): the existing film bible says (project file `projects/andre-time-travel/03_bibles/scene-bibles/caesar.md`, line 8): "Private atrium in a Roman domus / villa on the Palatine Hill". The ancient evidence found here puts Caesar's official residence on the Via Sacra at the Forum edge (Suet. 46.1), and says the pontifex maximus's residence moved to the Palatine only under Augustus (S07). I did not find a source placing Caesar in a Palatine house in early 44. I also did not search for the other possibility (a private Palatine property of Caesar's in 44); absence of evidence is not proof. The film may keep a fictional or Palatine setting as a creative choice, but the sheet should label it as such. This research does not decide.
- Other documented dwellings: modest house in the Subura earlier in life (Suet. 46.1); a country house at Nemi he demolished (Suet. 46.1). Neither is the early-44 setting.

### 2.2 Floors of an aristocratic domus in the 40s BCE
- Caesar and mosaics (DOC, anecdotal): Suet. Iul. 46.1: "he carried tesselated and mosaic floors about with him on his campaigns" (Loeb "tesselated and mosaic"; Thayer's note d says the Latin tessellata et sectilia pavimenta means mosaic floors and sectile pavements, and that "tessellated" and "mosaic" in the Loeb is a translation error/redundancy). Status: fetched-verified. This shows that fine pavements were a known Caesar taste; it does not say what was in the Domus Publica.
- Construction and finishes, S06 Vitruvius 7.1.1 (Gwilt trans.; Vitruvius wrote c. 30-15 BCE, so contemporary-adjacent): "I shall begin with pavements, which are the principal of the finishings". 7.1.3-4 describes layers (rubble, then a potsherd-and-lime upper layer of "not less than six inches"), over which "the pavement is laid to rule and level, whether composed of slabs or of tesseræ." Mentions Tiburtine tiles. Status: fetched-verified.
- Opus signinum (crushed-pottery lime mortar): S14 Wikipedia "Opus signinum" (secondary) quotes Pliny NH 35.165: broken pottery "beaten to powder, and tempered with lime ... forming the cement known as the Signine composition, so extensively employed for even making the pavements of houses." The article also says "While some signinum pavements have been found in Rome, the technique is not common there" and "The trend began in the 1st century BC, proliferating in private homes". Cites Tsakirgis AJA 94 (1990) and Joyce AJA 83 (1979). Status: fetched-verified (page text), secondary. The "not common in Rome" statement is relevant: most evidence for domus floors comes from Pompeii/Campania, not Rome.
- Pompeii examples (GENERIC, Campanian, not Rome): S15 Wikipedia "House of Sallust": "The atrium (cavaedium) featured opus signinum floor pavement with painted Ionic columns supporting the roof"; walls painted in First Style marble imitation. S16 Wikipedia "House of the Faun": "Unlike most Pompeian pavements of the late second and early first centuries, this mosaic is made of tesserae, and not the more common opus signinum". Both fetched-verified, secondary.
- Beaten earth, terracotta, marble: beaten earth floors are mentioned in a search summary for service/utility spaces (search-snippet-only, unattributed hobbyist/blog sources: not relied on). Terracotta tile and marble slab floors: Vitruvius 7.1 names slabs and Tiburtine tiles generally; I found no source dating marble room floors in Roman domus to the 40s BCE specifically. Marble floors in Caesar's house: NONE.
- Smith "Domus" (S10) describes Pompeian atria with "white tesserae, spotted with black" and an entrance "paved with mosaic" (from Pompeian examples; later period, 19th-c. reading). GENERIC.
- Winter dining floor, S06 Vitruvius 7.4.4-5 (c. 30-15 BCE; described as "pavements used in the Grecian winter rooms"): floor "excavated to the depth of about two feet" and filled with "a composition of pounded coals, lime, sand, and ashes ... The surface being then rubbed with stone, it has the appearance of a black pavement." Status: fetched-verified. GENERIC and explicitly Greek-style; not shown for Caesar.
- Summary for the shot: the evidence supports a finished (signinum or tessellated) floor in a wealthy house as GENERIC; the exact type for the Domus Publica is NONE.

### 2.3 Room features relevant to winter
- S06 Vitruvius 6.4.1: "Winter triclinia and baths are to face the winter west, because the afternoon light is wanted in them". Bed chambers and libraries towards the east. Status: fetched-verified. GENERIC (architect's recommendation).
- S06 Vitruvius 6.3.2: the displuviatum atrium, "useful for winter apartments, because the compluvium being upright, the light of the triclinia is not obstructed." fetched-verified. GENERIC.

## 3. Hair as seen from behind; neck and grooming

### 3.1 Hair, baldness and the comb-forward (DOC)
- S01 Suet. Iul. 45.2, verbatim: "He was somewhat overnice in the care of his person, being not only carefully trimmed and shaved, but even having superfluous hair plucked out, as some have charged; while his baldness was a disfigurement which troubled him greatly, since he found that it was often the subject of the gibes of his detractors. Because of it he used to comb forward his scanty locks from the crown of his head, and of all the honours voted him by the senate and people there was none which he received or made use of more gladly than the privilege of wearing a laurel wreath at all times." Status: fetched-verified. DOC.
- Reading for a rear view (my gloss): Suetonius places the visible thinning on top/front and says the remaining hair was brushed forward from the crown. That implies the back and nape hair was not the thin part, but Suetonius does not describe the nape. Nape length, curl, colour: NONE.
- S03 Dio 43.43.1, verbatim: "adorned with the laurel crown always and everywhere alike. The excuse that he gave for it was that his forehead was bald; yet he gave occasion for talk by this very circumstance that at that time, though well past youth, he still bestowed attention upon his appearance." Status: fetched-verified. DOC. Dio and Suetonius agree on the motive (concealing baldness).
- Hair colour, grey/white, or hair length at the neck: NONE. Ancient portrait heads (not consulted here; they show faces and are outside this brief) are the only visual source for the comb-forward fringe.

### 3.2 Grooming: shaving, barber, depilation (DOC + GENERIC)
- Suet. Iul. 45.2 as above: "carefully trimmed and shaved, but even having superfluous hair plucked out". DOC. This is a documented clean-shaven, groomed man; a barber/tonsor is implied but not named.
- S13 Smith "Barba" (GENERIC): Romans shaved from c. 300 BCE (Varro, Pliny VII.59); Scipio Africanus the first to shave daily; in the later republic "many ... shaved the beard only partially, and trimmed it". Fetched-verified. Barber's shears (forfex, S13b Smith "Forfex": "in cutting hair"). Razor details, nape shaving: NONE.
- Back-of-neck exposure: with the toga drawn up it is described in the death scenes: Plut. Caes. 66.6, verbatim: "Tullius seized his toga with both hands and pulled it down from his neck." (S04, DOC, fetched-verified). Also Plut. Caes. 60.6 (S04): "drawing back his toga from his neck, cried in loud tones to his friends that he was ready to offer his throat". Both show the toga rested on the neck/shoulders and could be drawn off the neck (DOC).

### 3.3 Laurel wreath: what is documented and what is not
- Documented as a privilege: Suet. 45.2 and Dio 43.43.1 as above. DOC. Both say "at all times" / "always and everywhere alike". Whether he wore it in his own house during private talks: NONE (the sources speak of public wearing; "at all times" is Suetonius's phrasing).
- A different object: Plut. Caes. 61.5 (S04): Antony "carried a diadem, round which a wreath of laurel was tied". Suet. Iul. 79.1 (S01): statue crowned with "a laurel wreath with a white fillet tied to it". These are diadem-plus-wreath objects on Lupercalia/Latin festival episodes, not Caesar's everyday wreath. The fillet (diadem) is white. DOC for the episodes; do not conflate with the privilege wreath.
- How the corona laurea was tied or knotted at the back of the head: NONE in ancient text. Smith "Corona" (S11) describes the triumphal crown only as "made with laurel or bay leaves" worn "around the head of the commander". It says nothing about ties. Search for Augustan portrait iconography found a German-language article (search-snippet-only) saying that a laurel wreath WITH a ribbon (tainia) is the most common form on Augustus's coinage and portraits. That is about Augustus, not Caesar; ribbon-end placement could not be confirmed. Caesar's lifetime denarii (RRC 480, "CAESAR DICT PERPETVO") show him laureate (Numista catalogue descriptions, search-snippet-only, "head right wearing laurel-wreath"); the pages did not mention ribbon ends. Any ribbon knot or hanging ends at the nape should be treated as unsupported for Caesar; production would need a specialist source (e.g. Bergmann, "Der Kranz des Kaisers", 2010: titled in a search result, content not read) or a coin/bust image inspected for the rear.
- Size, species (bay laurel, Laurus nobilis), and "closed circlet vs open ends": NONE for Caesar. Smith's "Corona Tonsa" distinction (leaves only) is generic and not tied to Caesar.

## 4. Collar, sleeves, drape

### 4.1 Tunic with long fringed sleeves, loose girdle (DOC)
- S01 Suet. Iul. 45.3, verbatim: "They say, too, that he was remarkable in his dress; that he wore a senator's tunic with fringed sleeves reaching to the wrist, and always had a girdle over it, though rather a loose one; and this, they say, was the occasion of Sulla's mot, when he often warned the nobles to keep an eye on the ill-girt boy." Status: fetched-verified. DOC. Note "They say" (ferunt): Suetonius reports tradition, writing c. 120 CE.
- Thayer's notes on that page (S01): note 43 "Latus clavus, the broad purple stripe ... All senators had the right to wear this; the peculiarity in Caesar's case consisted in the long fringed sleeves." Note 44: "it was not usual to wear [a girdle] with the latus clavus; Quint. XI.3.138. The looseness of the girdle was an additional peculiarity."
- Dio 43.43.2 (S03) corroborates "rather loose clothing" (see 1.1) and 43.43.4: "Sulla had looked askance at the looseness of his girdle". DOC. Two sources, but both late.
- S20 Smith "Tunica", verbatim: "It was considered a mark of effeminacy for men to wear Tunics with long sleeves (manicatae) and reaching to the feet (talares) (Cic. Cat. II.10). Julius Caesar was accustomed to wear one which had sleeves, with fringes at the wrist (ad manus fimbriata, Suet. Caes. 45)". fetched-verified.
- Tunic colour, fabric, length, sleeve cut, fringe colour, width of the broad stripe (latus clavus): the stripe is purple per Thayer's note and Quintilian (11.3.139 "If we wear the purple stripe"). Body colour (undyed/white wool is the GENERIC norm, not stated for Caesar): NONE for Caesar.
- Quintilian 11.3.138 (S21, 1st c. CE, GENERIC/RANK): "The speaker who has not the right to wear the broad stripe, will wear his girdle in such a way that the front edges of the tunic fall a little below his knees, while the edges in rear reach to the middle of his hams." Implication: a latus clavus tunic is worn ungirded-looking/long (Quintilian treats the girdle as for those WITHOUT the broad stripe). Fetched-verified.

### 4.2 Toga: drape from behind, left shoulder, umbo
- S22 Smith "Toga" (GENERIC for the republic, 19th-c. reconstruction from statues and Quintilian): "First, one corner is thrown over the left shoulder; then the other part of the garment is placed on the right shoulder, thus entirely covering the back and the right side up to the neck. It is then passed over the front of the body ... The remaining end, or corner, is then thrown back over the left shoulder, in such a manner as to cover the greater part of the arm." Also (the ancient mode): "The back of the figure ... was simply covered with the part of the garment which was drawn across it, and which, in the ancient mode of wearing it, reached down to the heels (Quintil. § 143)." Fetched-verified. The right arm is covered unless the toga is thrown off the right shoulder.
- Umbo, same page: "a mass of folds is seen in the middle of the part of the toga drawn across the chest (velut balteus). This is the umbo mentioned by Tertullian (de Pallio, 5)". The umbo is chest-front, so it is not visible from behind; only the balteus (diagonal band) crosses the back at the shoulder.
- S21 Quintilian 11.3.137-143 (GENERIC/RANK, c. 95 CE, so a later fashion): "The ancients, for example, wore no folds, and their successors wore them very short." "The toga itself should ... be round, and cut to fit ... Its front edge should by preference reach to the middle of the shin, while the back should be higher in proportion as the girdle is higher behind than in front." "The ancients used to let the toga fall to the heels". Also 11.3.143: Pliny claimed Cicero wore his toga long "to conceal his varicose veins" while "this fashion is to be seen in the statues of persons who lived after Cicero's day." Fetched-verified. Relevant to the 40s BCE: the fuller Imperial sinus drape postdates Caesar; a plainer, shorter-fold toga is the better-supported fit for 44.
- Caesar and the toga, back/neck mechanics (DOC, assassination accounts, outside the film's time but about toga behaviour): Suet. 82.1 "Cimber caught his toga by both shoulders"; Suet. 82.2 "he muffled his head in his robe, and at the same time drew down its lap to his feet with his left hand, in order to fall more decently, with the lower part of his body also covered"; Plut. Caes. 66.12 "he pulled his toga down over his head". Plut. 60.6 above. All fetched-verified. This shows a lap/fold (the sinus) that reaches the feet and is held with the left hand.
- Rank garb, public: toga praetexta (white, broad purple border): S22 Smith "Toga": "The toga praetexta had a broad purple border. It was worn ... by magistrates ... [and] the dictator". Caesar also had the "triumphal dress" voted for ordinary wear: Dio 44.4.2 (above) and Dio 43.43.1 ("he wore the triumphal garb, by decree, at all the games"). The triumphal dress was a purple toga picta; S22 Smith: "The toga picta ... was worn by generals in triumphs". Cic. Phil. 2.85 on a "toga purpurea" at the Lupercalia: could not verify in this session. Plut. 61.4 ("arrayed in triumphal attire") and Dio 44.11.2 ("the royal apparel") cover Lupercalia dress. So a private scene does NOT need the triumphal or royal dress; those are documented for public occasions. DOC for the grants, NONE for Caesar's choice at home.
- Garb "that the kings had once used": Dio 44.6.1: documented as granted. Description of it: NONE.

### 4.3 Colours: undyed wool, purple border, Tyrian purple
- Toga colour: S22 Smith: "The colour of the toga worn by men (toga virilis) was generally white, that is, the natural colour of white wool." Fetched-verified. GENERIC. "toga candida" chalk-whitened was for office candidates; "toga pulla" (dark) for mourning.
- Purple: S24 Pliny NH 9.60 (Perseus numbering; Bostock/Riley trans.), verbatim: "It is for this colour that the fasces and the axes of Rome make way in the crowd; it is this that asserts the majesty of childhood; it is this that distinguishes the senator from the man of equestrian rank; by persons arrayed in this colour are prayers addressed to propitiate the gods; on every garment it sheds a lustre, and in the triumphal vestment it is to be seen mingled with gold." Fetched-verified. RANK/GENERIC.
- Hue: Pliny NH 9.62 (Perseus numbering), verbatim: "It is considered of the best quality when it has exactly the colour of clotted blood, and is of a blackish hue to the sight, but of a shining appearance when held up to the light". Fetched-verified. Also: "The tint that inclines to red is looked upon as inferior to that which is of a blackish hue." So the prized Tyrian hue is dark blood-red/blackish, not bright violet. (Pliny 1st c. CE; GENERIC.)
- Price and chronology: Pliny NH 9.63 quoting Cornelius Nepos: "the violet purple was in favour, a pound of which used to sell at one hundred denarii; and not long after, the Tarentine red was all the fashion. This last was succeeded by the Tyrian dibapha, which could not be bought for even one thousand denarii per pound. P. Lentulus Spinther, the curule ædile, was the first who used the dibapha for the prætexta, and he was greatly censured for it". Spinther's aedileship is dated to 63 BCE (Pliny: "in the consulship of Cicero ... 691 AUC"). So double-dyed Tyrian was a luxury and controversial for a praetexta border in the 60s, which is relevant background for the praetexta border colour in 44. Fetched-verified. The border colour of Caesar's own garments: NONE beyond "purple".

## 5. Hands

### 5.1 Signet ring
- Caesar's ring (DOC): S03 Dio 43.43.3, verbatim: "Accordingly he used also to wear a carven image of her [Venus] in full armour on his ring and he made her name his watchword in almost all the greatest dangers." Status: fetched-verified. DOC, single late source. It states "his ring", singular, with an engraved Venus Armata (armed Venus); it does not say which hand or finger, metal, or gem. (Suet. Iul. 47.1 independently says he was "an enthusiastic collector of gems, carvings, statues".) Gem colour, mount, size: NONE.
- Which hand and finger (GENERIC): S23 Gellius NA 10.10.1 (Rolfe trans.), verbatim: "I have heard that the ancient Greeks wore a ring on the finger of the left hand which is next to the little finger. They say, too, that the Roman men commonly wore their rings in that way." Fetched-verified. So left hand, fourth finger, is the customary placement (GENERIC). Smith "Annulus" (S25) agrees "Rings were mostly worn on the fourth finger" and notes that by the late republic the gold ring was a privilege of "senators, magistrates, and equites". Note: Gellius wrote c. 170 CE, citing older custom ("ancient Greeks and Romans"); the custom for 44 BCE is probable but not independently dated. Caesar's actual finger: NONE.
- Quintilian 11.3.142 (S21): "The hand should not be overloaded with rings, which should under no circumstances encroach upon the middle joint of the finger." GENERIC etiquette for orators; supports one or two rings, not stacked, sitting below the middle knuckle.
- Sealing practice: Smith "Annulus": rings "were the same as those for which we use our seals" and homes were sealed against theft; the ring as state seal is imperial. GENERIC.

### 5.2 Writing: stylus, wax tablets, diptych
- Caesar and the stylus (DOC): S01 Suet. Iul. 82.2: "Caesar caught Casca's arm and ran it through with his stylus". Thayer's note 74: "A pointed instrument of bone or metal, for writing on waxen tablets." Status: fetched-verified. Suetonius calls it a stylus/graphium (Smith "Stilus" S26 cites Suet. Jul. 82 for graphium). So Caesar carried a stylus on his person at the Senate on the Ides. DOC.
- Tablets: S27 Smith "Tabulae": "Tabulae and Tabellae more frequently signify waxen tablets (tabulae ceratae), which were thick pieces of wood usually of an oblong shape, covered over with wax (cera). The wax was written on by means of the stilus." Two tablets fastened are "Diptycha"; "pugillares ... small enough to be held in the hand". Fetched-verified. S26 "Stilus": "At one end it was sharpened to a point for scratching the characters upon the wax (Quintil. I.1 §27), while the other end being flat and circular served to render the surface of the tablets smooth again". Fetched-verified. GENERIC.
- Papyrus/ink for letters: Suet. Iul. 56.6 (S01): "Some letters of his to the senate are also preserved, and he seems to have been the first to reduce such documents to pages and the form of a note-book, whereas previously consuls and generals sent their reports written right across the sheet." And he wrote private letters in a cipher shifting by four letters. DOC. This is about letters/documents, not his hand.
- Dictation (DOC): Plut. Caes. 17.4 (S04): on journeys "one slave who was accustomed to write from dictation as he travelled sitting by his side"; 17.7 "he practised dictating letters on horseback and keeping two scribes at once busy". Fetched-verified. So much of Caesar's text was dictated to scribes, not written by his own hand. Whether he wrote with his right or left hand: NONE.
- How a man of about 55 wrote, posture, grip, reading distance: NONE specific to Caesar or to age. Pliny's study habits and the "tablet on the knee" posture were not verified here; do not assert. A search for Vindolanda (c. 100 CE, Roman Britain) was done only as a generic check; the British Museum and general descriptions (search-snippet-only) confirm wax stylus tablets with a pointed iron stylus and a flat eraser end, and ink leaf tablets, but they are 150 years later and provincial, so GENERIC at best.

### 5.3 Health: epilepsy, fainting, headaches (note only; not for dramatisation)
- S01 Suet. Iul. 45.1, verbatim: "sound of health, except that towards the end he was subject to sudden fainting fits and to nightmare as well. He was twice attacked by the falling sickness during his campaigns." Thayer's note 42 glosses falling sickness as epilepsy (morbus comitialis). DOC. Status: fetched-verified. Note: "towards the end" places the fainting fits late in life, i.e. around the period of the film.
- S04 Plut. Caes. 17.2, verbatim: "he was of a spare habit, had a soft and white skin, suffered from distemper in the head, and was subject to epileptic fits, a trouble which first attacked him, we are told, in Corduba." DOC. Fetched-verified. (Plutarch's "spare habit" means lean build.)
- S04 Plut. Caes. 60.6-8: Caesar, after receiving the senate without rising, "made his disease an excuse ... saying that the senses of those who are thus afflicted do not usually remain steady when they address a multitude standing, but are speedily shaken and whirled about, bringing on giddiness and insensibility. However, what he said was not true". Plutarch doubts it. Suet. 78 and Dio 44.8.3 give alternative explanations (Balbus holding him back; claimed diarrhoea). The ancient sources disagree on whether illness explained the seated reception.
- Tremor, hand shaking, handedness, eyesight, pallor of the hands: NONE.

## 6. Plausible light sources (options only; NOT a lighting design)

All items below are plausible, documented period options. None is shown to have been used in Caesar's own rooms in Jan-Mar 44. Interior lighting of the Domus Publica: NONE.

- Oil lamps (lucernae), GENERIC. S28 Smith "Lucerna": "an oil lamp. The Greeks and the Romans originally used candles; but in later times candles were chiefly confined to the houses of the lower classes." "the greater part of [the lamps] are made of terra cotta ... but also a considerable number of bronze" and "The lamps sometimes hung in chains from the ceiling of the room ... but generally stood upon a stand. [Candelabrum]". Wicks (ellychnia), multiple nozzles (monomyxos, dimyxos etc.). "The lucernae cubiculares were burnt in bed-chambers all night". "Perfumed oil was sometimes burnt in the lamps". Status: fetched-verified. Museum anchor: S29 Met object 74.51.2091, terracotta oil lamp, "1st century BCE-1st century CE", Roman, Early Imperial (metadata via the Met open-access API, fetched-verified; I did not read the full description). Other Met lamps in the 74.51.xxxx series are dated 40-100 CE or later. A true Late Republican (c. 44 BCE) museum lamp was not located; flag as a gap. Smith's statement about candles being lower-class is an 1875 generalisation; see below.
- Candles (candelae), GENERIC. S30 Smith "Candela": "a candle, made either of wax (cerea) or tallow (sebacea), was used universally by the Romans before the invention of oil lamps". S31 Wikipedia "Candle" (secondary) says the opposite emphasis: "Romans made true dipped candles from tallow and beeswax. Beeswax candles were expensive and their use was limited to the wealthy, so oil lamps were the more commonly used lighting devices in Roman times." These two secondary sources disagree on who used candles, so the safe statement is: tallow and wax candles both existed; lamps were the common source. Smith "Candelabrum" (S32): "CANDELABRUM, was originally a candlestick, but was afterwards used to support lamps"; "most common kind were made of wood ... those found in Herculaneum and Pompeii are mostly of bronze" (S32). Fetched-verified. Candle use in Caesar's house: NONE.
- Braziers (foculi), GENERIC. S33 Smith "Focus": "FOCUS, dim. FOCULUS ... a fire-place; a hearth; a brazier ... it was also frequently made of bronze ... This movable-hearth, or brazier, was properly called foculus ... carried continually from place to place. One is shown at p190. Another, found at Caere in Etruria, and preserved in the British Museum". Fetched-verified. Heating use at Pompeii: bronze braziers from Pompeii in Naples museum are mentioned in search summaries (search-snippet-only; some hits were modern replicas or dealer pages and were discarded). Charcoal braziers give a low orange glow and need ventilation: Vitruvius 7.4.4 (S06): "In winter triclinia, neither large pictures nor delicate ornaments in the cornice, under the vault, are to be introduced, because they are soon injured by the smoke of the fire, and of the quantity of lights used therein." Fetched-verified. That one sentence supports, for winter dining rooms, both a fire (smoke) and "a quantity of lights" (many lamps) together, and explains the black polished lower walls "above the podium ... polished pannels of a black colour".
- Compluvium daylight, GENERIC. S06 Vitruvius 6.3.2 (above) on the displuviatum atrium preserving light for triclinia in winter apartments; S10 Smith "Domus" on the compluvium: "a large apartment roofed over with the exception of an opening in the centre, called compluvium, towards which the roof sloped so as to throw the rain-water into a cistern in the floor, termed impluvium". Fetched-verified. Winter daylight through an atrium opening is cold, and rain enters the impluvium: no source specific to early 44.
- Shuttered or closed windows, GENERIC. S06 Vitruvius 6.4.2: "Spring and autumn triclinia should be towards the east, for then, if the windows be closed till the sun has passed the meridian, they are cool at the time they are wanted for use." Fetched-verified. Window glass/shutter details for Rome in 44: NONE (not found).
- Torches: Caesar mounted the Capitol at his Gallic triumph "by torchlight, with forty elephants bearing lamps on his right and his left" (Suet. Iul. 37.2, DOC, 46 BCE). Outdoor/ceremonial.
- Night in the house: Plut. Caes. 63.8: before the Ides "all the windows and doors of the chamber flew open at once, and Caesar ... noticed ... the noise and the light of the moon shining down upon him" (DOC, but literary/portent; indicates a bedroom with openings that admit moonlight).

## 7. Gaps and uncertainties (what is NOT found)

1. Shoes: Caesar's exact footwear is documented only by one sentence in Dio ("high and of a reddish colour", "later on", "sometimes"). No source for indoor or domestic footwear for Caesar. No shape, lacing, sole, hobnails, or height beyond "high". Dio does not say "boots".
2. Residence: Suetonius 46.1 supports the Via Sacra official residence (domus publica, probable per Platner-Ashby). The film bible's "Palatine" location is unsupported by anything found. Interior of the domus publica: no description anywhere found. Not resolved; creative choice for the production.
3. Floor: no source for the floor of Caesar's house. Evidence is Pompeian/Campanian and later literature. Opus signinum is "not common" in Rome per the cited scholarship. Marble: no 40s BCE support found.
4. Hair from behind: nape, length at the back, and grey are not described. The only documented facts are baldness at the crown/forehead, thin hair combed forward from the crown, and shaving/trimming/plucking.
5. Laurel wreath: no ancient text on tying, knots, ribbons, or whether he wore it in his house. Ribbon-end placement unsupported for Caesar. The privilege is documented; its form is not.
6. Tunic: fringed sleeves to the wrist and loose girdle are documented. Colour, fabric, fringe colour, and stripe width are not. The latus clavus is purple per the secondary note and Quintilian.
7. Toga: no Caesar-specific cut or drape. Smith's and Quintilian's accounts reflect statues and a later fashion. A Caesar-era toga was plainer, with a shorter sinus (Smith/Quintilian), but this is reconstruction.
8. Ring: one ring with engraved armed Venus (Dio). Hand/finger customs from Gellius (c. 170 CE). Caesar's own finger: not found.
9. Writing: stylus on person (Suet. 82), dictation to scribes (Plut. 17). No source on his grip, hand, or age effects. Handedness: not found. No source mentions a tremor. Epilepsy/fainting/headaches are documented; no ancient source ties them to his hands or posture.
10. Light: all items are generic. No museum lamp securely dated to the 40s BCE was located; the Met lamp I verified is dated 1st c. BCE-1st c. CE. Candle/lamp class split is disputed between the two secondary sources.
11. Sella curulis: the object form is documented generically (Smith; Wikipedia on X-shaped curved legs); backs, arms, cushion, and gilding details are not agreed. No source says Caesar sat on one in a private conversation.
12. Source quality: Suetonius and Dio are 1.5-2.5 centuries after the events; Plutarch about 150 years. Smith (1875) and Wikipedia are secondary. No archaeological catalogue of the Domus Publica interior was found.
13. Not done: no Cicero letters or Appian read; no Met/British Museum object pages other than the Met lamp metadata; no direct access to Bergmann, Crawford RRC 480 plates, or Tsakirgis/Joyce articles; no reading of Pliny the Younger (window shutters) or Martial.
14. Search-snippet-only items are labelled in the table and must not be used as proof.
