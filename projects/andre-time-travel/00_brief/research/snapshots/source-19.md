1939-1945](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945/retrospect.htm)<br>The next break in the weather over Japan was due to appear<br>just three days after the<br>[attack on Hiroshima](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945/hiroshima.htm), to be followed by at<br>least five more days of prohibitive weather.  The<br>[plutonium implosion bomb](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Science/BombDesign/implosion.html), nicknamed<br>"Fat Man," was rushed into readiness to take<br>advantage of this window.  No further orders were<br>required for the attack. <br>[Truman's order of July 25th](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945/potsdam_decision.htm) had<br>authorized the dropping of additional bombs as soon as<br>they were ready.  At 3:47 a.m. on August 9, 1945, a<br>B-29 named _Bock's Car_ lifted off from Tinian and<br>headed toward the primary target: Kokura Arsenal, a<br>massive collection of war industries adjacent to the city<br>of Kokura.<br> <br>[![Flight paths for Hiroshima and Nagasaki missions](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/MissionMap1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/mission_map_image.htm) From this point on, few things went according to<br>plan.  The aircraft commander, Major Charles W.<br>Sweeney, ordered the arming of the bomb only ten minutes<br>after take-off so that the aircraft could be pressurized<br>and climb above the lightning and squalls that menaced the<br>flight all the way to Japan.  (A journalist, William<br>L. Laurence of the _New York Times_, on an<br>escorting aircraft saw some "St. Elmo's fire"<br>glowing on the edges of the aircraft and worried that the<br>static electricity might detonate the bomb.)  Sweeney<br>then discovered that due to a minor malfunction he would<br>not be able to access his reserve fuel.  The aircraft<br>next had to orbit over Yaku-shima off the south coast of<br>Japan for almost an hour in order to rendezvous with its<br>two escort B-29s, one of which never did arrive.  The<br>weather had been reported satisfactory earlier in the day<br>over Kokura Arsenal, but by the time the B-29 finally<br>arrived there, the target was obscured by smoke and<br>haze.  Two more passes over the target still produced<br>no sightings of the aiming point.  As an aircraft<br>crewman, Jacob Beser, later recalled, Japanese fighters<br>and bursts of antiaircraft fire were by this time starting<br>to make things "a little hairy."  Kokura no<br>longer appeared to be an option, and there was only enough<br>fuel on board to return to the secondary airfield on<br>Okinawa, making one hurried pass as they went over their<br>secondary target, the<br>[city of Nagasaki](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Places/Other/nagasaki.html).  As Beser later<br>put it, "there was no sense dragging the bomb home or<br>dropping it in the ocean."<br>[![Fat Man at Tinian Island, August 1945](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/FatMan1.gif)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/fat_man_image.htm) As it turned out, cloud cover obscured Nagasaki as<br>well.  Sweeney reluctantly approved a much less<br>accurate radar approach on the target.  At the last<br>moment the bombardier, Captain Kermit K. Beahan, caught a<br>brief glimpse of the city's stadium through the clouds and<br>dropped the bomb.  At 11:02 a.m., at an altitude of<br>1,650 feet, Fat Man (right) [exploded](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Processes/BombTesting/explosion.html) over<br>Nagasaki.  The [yield](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Processes/BombTesting/yield.html) of the<br>explosion was later estimated at 21 kilotons, 40 percent<br>greater than that of the<br>[Hiroshima bomb](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Processes/BombTesting/assembly.html).<br>[![Mitsubishi-Urakami Torpedo Works, 1,400 feet north of ground zero, Nagasaki.  Torpedoes used in the attack on Pearl Harbor were built here.](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/NagasakiMitsubishi1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/mitsubishi_image.htm) Nagasaki was an industrial center and major port on the<br>western coast of Kyushu.  As had happened at<br>Hiroshima, the "all-clear" from an early morning<br>air raid alert had long been given by the time the B-29<br>had begun its bombing run.  A small conventional raid<br>on Nagasaki on August 1st had resulted in a partial<br>evacuation of the city, especially of school<br>children.  There were still almost 200,000 people in<br>the city below the bomb when it exploded.  The<br>hurriedly-targeted weapon ended up detonating almost<br>exactly between two of the principal targets in the city,<br>the Mitsubishi Steel and Arms Works to the south, and the<br>Mitsubishi-Urakami Torpedo Works (left) to the<br>north.  Had the bomb exploded farther south the<br>residential and commercial heart of the city would have<br>suffered much greater damage.<br> <br>[![Devestation at Nagasaki (a Roman Catholic church is visible in the distance).](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/NagasakiDestruction1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/nagasaki_destruction_image.htm) In general, though Fat Man exploded with greater force<br>than Little Boy, the damage at Nagasaki was not as great<br>as it had been at Hiroshima.  The hills of Nagasaki,<br>its geographic layout, and the bomb's detonation over an<br>industrial area all helped shield portions of the city<br>from the weapon's [blast](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Processes/BombTesting/blast.html),<br>[heat](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Processes/BombTesting/thermal-radiation.html), and<br>[radiation](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Processes/BombTesting/nuclear-radiation.html) effects.  The explosion<br>affected a total area of approximately 43 square<br>miles.  About 8.5 of those square miles were water,<br>and 33 more square miles were only partially<br>settled.  Many roads and rail lines escaped major<br>damage.  In some areas electricity was not knocked<br>out, and fire breaks created over the last several months<br>helped to prevent the spread of fires to the<br>south.<br>[![Mother and child receive rice from emergency relief party, one mile southeast of ground zero, Nagasaki, morning of August 10, 1945.](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/NagasakiMotherChild1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/nagasaki_mother_child_image.htm) Although the destruction at Nagasaki has generally<br>received less worldwide attention than that at Hiroshima,<br>it was extensive nonetheless.  Almost everything up<br>to half a mile from ground zero was completely destroyed,<br>including even the earthquake-hardened concrete structures<br>that had sometimes survived at comparable distances at<br>Hiroshima.  According to a Nagasaki Prefectural<br>report "men and animals died almost instantly"<br>within 1 kilometer (0.62 miles) of the point of<br>detonation.  Almost all homes within a mile and a<br>half were destroyed, and dry, combustible materials such<br>as paper instantly burst into flames as far away as 10,000<br>feet from ground zero.  Of the 52,000 homes in<br>Nagasaki, 14,000 were destroyed and 5,400 more seriously<br>damaged.  Only 12 percent of the homes escaped<br>unscathed.  The official Manhattan Engineer District<br>report on the attack termed the damage to the two<br>Mitsubishi plants "spectacular."  Despite<br>the absence of a firestorm, numerous secondary fires<br>erupted throughout the city.  Fire-fighting efforts<br>were hampered by water line breaks, and six weeks later<br>the city was still suffering from a shortage of<br>water.  A U.S. Navy officer who visited the city in<br>mid-September reported that, even over a month after the<br>attack, "a smell of death and corruption pervades the<br>place."  As at Hiroshima, the psychological<br>effects of the attack were undoubtedly<br>considerable.<br>[![Bodies in a trench, Nagasaki](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/NagasakiDead1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/nagasaki_dead_image.htm) As with the estimates of deaths at Hiroshima, it will<br>never be known for certain how many people died as a<br>result of the atomic attack on Nagasaki.  The best<br>estimate is 40,000 people died initially, with 60,000 more<br>injured.  By January 1946, the number of deaths<br>probably approached 70,000, with perhaps ultimately twice<br>that number dead total within five years.  For those<br>areas of Nagasaki affected by the explosion, the death<br>rate was comparable to that at Hiroshima.<br> <br>The day after the attack on Nagasaki, the emperor of Japan<br>overruled the military leaders of Japan and forced them to<br>offer to<br>[surrender (almost) unconditionally](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945/surrender.htm).<br> <br>- [The War Enters Its Final Phase,
