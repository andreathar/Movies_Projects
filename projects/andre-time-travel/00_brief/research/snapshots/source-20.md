1939-1945](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945/retrospect.htm)<br>Prior to the atomic attacks on<br>[Hiroshima](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945/hiroshima.htm) and<br>[Nagasaki](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945/nagasaki.htm), elements existed within the Japanese government that<br>were trying to find a way to end the war.  In June<br>and July 1945, Japan attempted to enlist the help of the<br>Soviet Union to serve as an intermediary in<br>negotiations.  No direct communication occurred with<br>the United States about peace talks, but American leaders<br>knew of these maneuvers because the United States for a<br>long time had been intercepting and decoding many internal<br>Japanese diplomatic communications.  From these<br>intercepts, the United States learned that some within the<br>Japanese government advocated outright surrender.  A<br>few diplomats overseas cabled home to urge just<br>that.<br>From the replies these diplomats received from Tokyo, the<br>United States learned that anything Japan might agree to<br>would not be a surrender so much as a "negotiated peace"<br>involving numerous conditions.  These conditions<br>probably would require, at a minimum, that the Japanese<br>home islands remain unoccupied by foreign forces and even<br>allow Japan to retain some of its wartime conquests in<br>East Asia.  Many within the Japanese government were<br>extremely reluctant to discuss any concessions, which<br>would mean that a "negotiated peace" to them would only<br>amount to little more than a truce where the Allies agreed<br>to stop attacking Japan.  After twelve years of<br>Japanese military aggression against China and over three<br>and one-half years of war with the United States (begun<br>with the surprise attack on Pearl Harbor), American<br>leaders were reluctant to accept anything less than a<br>complete Japanese surrender.<br>[![Joseph Stalin, Harry Truman, and Winston Churchill at the Potsdam Conference, July 1945](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/Potsdam1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/potsdam_image.htm) The one possible exception to this was the personal<br>status of the emperor himself.  Although the Allies<br>had long been publicly demanding "unconditional<br>surrender," in private there had been some discussion of<br>exempting the emperor from war trials and allowing him to<br>remain as ceremonial head of state.  In the end, at<br>[Potsdam](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945/potsdam_decision.htm), the Allies (right) went with both a "carrot and a<br>stick," trying to encourage those in Tokyo who advocated<br>peace with assurances that Japan eventually would be<br>allowed to form its own government, while combining these<br>assurances with vague warnings of "prompt and utter<br>destruction" if Japan did not surrender immediately. <br>No explicit mention was made of the emperor possibly<br>remaining as ceremonial head of state.  Japan<br>publicly rejected the Potsdam Declaration, and on July 25,<br>1945, [President Harry S. Truman](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/People/Administrators/harry-truman.html) gave<br>[the order to commence atomic attacks](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945/potsdam_decision.htm#decision)<br>on Japan as soon as possible.<br>[![Mushroom cloud over Hiroshima, August 6, 1945](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/HiroshimaCloud1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/hiroshima_cloud_image.htm) Following the bombing of Hiroshima on August 6, 1945<br>(left), the Japanese government met to consider what to do<br>next.  The emperor had been urging since June that<br>Japan find some way to end the war, but the Japanese<br>Minister of War and the heads of both the Army and the<br>Navy held to their position that Japan should wait and see<br>if arbitration via the Soviet Union might still produce<br>something less than a surrender.  Military leaders<br>also hoped that if they could hold out until the ground<br>invasion of Japan began, they would be able to inflict so<br>many casualties on the Allies that Japan still might win<br>some sort of negotiated settlement.  Next came the<br>virtually simultaneous arrival of news of the Soviet<br>declaration of war on Japan of August 8, 1945, and the<br>atomic bombing of Nagasaki of the following day. <br>Another Imperial Council was held the night of August<br>9-10, and this time the vote on surrender was a tie,<br>3-to-3.  For the first time in a generation, the<br>emperor (right) stepped forward from his normally<br>ceremonial-only role and personally broke the tie,<br>ordering Japan to surrender.  On August 10, 1945,<br>Japan offered to surrender to the Allies, the only<br>condition being that the emperor be allowed to remain the<br>nominal head of state.<br>[![Emperor Hirohito](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/EmperorHirohito2.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/emperor_hirohito_image.htm) Planning for the use of additional nuclear weapons<br>continued even as these deliberations were ongoing. <br>On August 10, [Leslie Groves](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/People/Administrators/leslie-groves.html) reported to<br>the War Department that the next bomb, another<br>[plutonium implosion weapon](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Science/BombDesign/implosion.html), would be<br>"ready for delivery on the first suitable weather after 17<br>or 18 August."  Following the destruction of<br>Hiroshima and Nagasaki, only two targets remained from the<br>original list: Kokura Arsenal and the city of<br>Niigata.  Groves therefore requested that additional<br>targets be added to the target list.  His deputy,<br>General Kenneth Nichols, suggested<br>Tokyo.  Truman, however, ordered an immediate<br>halt to atomic attacks while surrender negotiations were<br>ongoing.  As the Secretary of Commerce Henry Wallace<br>recorded in his diary, Truman remarked that he did not<br>like the idea of killing "all those kids."<br>[![B-29s on a bombing run](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/B29sKorea1.gif)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/b29_image.htm) On August 12, the United States announced that it would<br>accept the Japanese surrender, making clear in its<br>statement that the emperor could remain in a purely<br>ceremonial capacity only.  Debate raged within the<br>Japanese government over whether to accept the American<br>terms or fight on.  Meanwhile, American leaders were<br>growing impatient, and on August 13 conventional air raids<br>resumed on Japan.  Thousands more Japanese civilians<br>died while their leaders delayed.  The Japanese<br>people learned of the surrender negotiations for the first<br>time when, on August 14, B-29s showered Tokyo with<br>thousands of leaflets containing translated copies of the<br>American reply of August 12.  Later that day, the<br>emperor called another meeting of his cabinet and<br>instructed them to accept the Allied terms immediately,<br>explaining "I cannot endure the thought of letting my<br>people suffer any longer"; if the war did not end "the<br>whole nation would be reduced to ashes."<br> <br>[![Aircraft fly in formation over the U.S.S. Missouri during the Japanese surrender ceremony, Tokyo Bay, September 2, 1945.](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/MissouriFlyover1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/missouri_flyover.htm) The only question remaining now was if Japan's military<br>leaders would allow the emperor to surrender. <br>Loyalty to the emperor was an absolute in the Japanese<br>military, but so was the refusal to surrender, and now<br>that the two had come into conflict, open rebellion was a<br>possible result.  The emperor recorded a message in<br>which he personally accepted the Allied surrender terms,<br>to be broadcast over Japanese radio the following<br>day.  This way everyone in Japan would know that<br>surrender was the emperor's personal will.  Some<br>within the Japanese military actually attempted to steal<br>this recording before it could be [![General Douglas MacArthur signs the Japanese surrender document, U.S.S. Missouri, Tokyo Bay, September 2, 1945.](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/MissouriMacArthur1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/general_macarthur.htm)<br>broadcast, while others attempted a more general military<br>coup in order to seize power and continue the war. <br>Other elements of the Japanese military remained loyal to<br>the emperor.  The Minister of War, General Anami<br>Korechika, personally supported continuing the war, but he<br>also could not bring himself to openly rebel against his<br>emperor.  The strength of his dilemma was such that<br>he opted for suicide as the only honorable way out. <br>In the end, his refusal to assist the coup plotters was<br>instrumental in their defeat by elements within the<br>military that remained loyal to the emperor.<br>[![Japanese prisoners hear Emperor Hirohito's announcement of the surrender of Japan, Guam, August 15, 1945.](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/SurrenderNews1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/surrender_image.htm) On August 15, 1945, the emperor's broadcast announcing<br>Japan's surrender was heard via radio all over<br>Japan.  For most of his subjects, it was the first<br>time that they had ever heard his voice.  The emperor<br>explained that "the war situation has developed not<br>necessarily to Japan's advantage," and that "the enemy has<br>begun to employ a new and most cruel bomb."  Over the<br>next few weeks, Japan and the United States worked out the<br>details of the surrender, and on September 2, 1945, the<br>formal surrender ceremony took place on the deck of the<br>U.S.S. _Missouri_.<br> <br>- [The
