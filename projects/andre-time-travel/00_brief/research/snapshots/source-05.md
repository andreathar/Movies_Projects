S-10,000](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/TrinityBunker1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/trinity_bunker_image.htm) Until the atomic bomb could be tested, doubt would remain<br>about its effectiveness.  The world had never seen a<br>nuclear explosion before, and estimates varied widely on<br>how much energy would be released.  Some<br>[scientists](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/People/Scientists/scientists.html) at<br>[Los Alamos](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Places/LosAlamos/la-lab.html) continued privately to have<br>doubts that it would work at all.  There was only<br>enough weapons-grade uranium available for one bomb, and<br>confidence in the [gun-type design](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Science/BombDesign/gun-type.html) was<br>high, so on July 14, 1945, most of the uranium bomb<br>("Little Boy") began its trip westward to the Pacific<br>without its design having ever been fully tested.  A<br>test of the plutonium bomb seemed vital, however, both to<br>confirm its novel [implosion](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Science/BombDesign/implosion.html) design and to<br>gather data on nuclear explosions in general. <br>Several plutonium bombs were now "in the pipeline" and<br>would be available over the next few weeks and<br>months.  It was therefore decided to test one of<br>these.<br>[![Map of the Trinity Test Site](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/TrinitySiteMap1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/trinity_map_image.htm)[Robert Oppenheimer](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/People/Administrators/robert-oppenheimer.html) chose to name this<br>the "Trinity" test, a name inspired by the poems of John<br>Donne.  The [site chosen](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Places/Other/trinity.html) was a remote<br>corner on the Alamagordo Bombing Range known as the<br>"Jornada del Muerto," or "Journey of Death," 210 miles<br>south of Los Alamos.  The elaborate instrumentation<br>surrounding the site was tested with an explosion of a<br>large amount of conventional explosives on May 7. <br>Preparations continued throughout May and June and were<br>complete by the beginning of July.  Three observation<br>bunkers located 10,000 yards north, west, and south<br>(right) of the firing tower at ground zero would attempt<br>to measure key aspects of the reaction. <br>Specifically, scientists would try to determine the<br>symmetry of the implosion and the amount of energy<br>released.  Additional measurements would be taken to<br>determine damage estimates, and equipment would record the<br>behavior of the [fireball](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Processes/BombTesting/explosion.html).  The<br>biggest concern was<br>[control of the radioactivity the test device would\<br>release](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945/trinity_safety.htm).  Not entirely content to trust favorable<br>meteorological conditions to carry the radioactivity into<br>the upper atmosphere, the Army stood ready to evacuate the<br>people in surrounding areas.<br>[![Gadget's core arrives at the Trinity site.](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/GadgetCar1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/gadget_car_image.htm) On July 12,  the plutonium core was taken to the<br>test area in an army sedan (left).  The non-nuclear<br>components left for the test site at 12:01 a.m., Friday<br>the 13th.  During the day on the 13th, final assembly<br>of the "Gadget" (as it was nicknamed) took place in the<br>McDonald ranch house.  By 5:00 p.m. on the 15th, the<br>device had been assembled and hoisted atop the 100-foot<br>firing tower.  [Leslie Groves](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/People/Administrators/leslie-groves.html),<br>[Vannevar Bush](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/People/Administrators/vannevar-bush.html),<br>[James Conant](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/People/Administrators/james-conant.html),<br>[Ernest Lawrence](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/People/Administrators/ernest-lawrence.html), Thomas Farrell,<br>[James Chadwick](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/People/Scientists/james-chadwick.html), and others arrived in the<br>test area, where it was pouring rain. [![Herb Lehr, SED, holding the Gadget's core, July 1945.](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/GadgetCoreLehr3.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/lehr_core_image.htm)<br>Groves and Oppenheimer, standing at the S-10,000 control<br>bunker, discussed what to do if the weather did not break<br>in time for the scheduled 4:00 a.m. test.  To break<br>the tension, Fermi began offering anyone listening a wager<br>on "whether or not the bomb would ignite the atmosphere,<br>and if so, whether it would merely destroy New Mexico or<br>destroy the world." Oppenheimer himself had bet ten<br>dollars against George Kistiakowsky's entire month's pay<br>that the bomb would not work at all.  Meanwhile,<br>Edward Teller was making everyone nervous by applying<br>liberal amounts of sunscreen in the pre-dawn darkness and<br>offering to pass it around.  At 3:30, Groves and<br>Oppenheimer pushed the time back to 5:30. At 4:00, the<br>rain stopped. Kistiakowsky and his team armed the device<br>shortly after 5:00 and retreated to S-10,000.  In<br>accordance with his policy that each observe from<br>different locations in case of an accident, Groves left<br>Oppenheimer and joined Bush and Conant at base camp. <br>Those in shelters heard the countdown over the public<br>address system, while observers at base camp picked it up<br>on an FM radio signal.<br>[![Gadget is hoisted up the tower.](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/GadgetHoisted1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/gadget_hoisted_image.htm) During the final seconds, most observers laid down on the<br>ground with their feet facing the Trinity site and simply<br>waited.  As the countdown approached one minute,<br>Isidore Rabi said to the man lying next to him, Kenneth<br>Griesen, "Aren't you nervous?"  "Nope" was Griesen's<br>reply.  As Groves later wrote, "As I lay there in the<br>final seconds, I thought only of what I would do if the<br>countdown got to zero and nothing happened."  Conant<br>said he never knew seconds could be so long.  As the<br>countdown reached 10 seconds, Griesen suddenly blurted out<br>to his neighbor Rabi, "Now I'm scared."  Three, two,<br>one, and Sam Allison cried out, "Now!"<br> <br>[![Unidentified man with the Gadget shortly before the test.](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/Gadget1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/gadget_image.htm) At precisely 5:30 a.m. on Monday, July 16, 1945, the<br>nuclear age began. While Manhattan Project staff members<br>watched anxiously, the device exploded over the New Mexico<br>desert, vaporizing the tower and turning the asphalt<br>around the base of the tower to green sand.  Seconds<br>after the explosion came a huge<br>[blast](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Processes/BombTesting/blast.html) wave and<br>[heat](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Processes/BombTesting/thermal-radiation.html) searing out across the desert. <br>No one could see the [radiation](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Processes/BombTesting/nuclear-radiation.html) generated<br>by the explosion, but they all knew it was there. <br>The steel container "Jumbo," weighing over 200 tons and<br>transported to the desert only to be eliminated from the<br>test, was knocked ajar even though it stood half a mile<br>from ground zero.  As the orange and yellow fireball<br>stretched up and spread, a second column, narrower than<br>the first, rose and flattened into a mushroom shape, thus<br>providing the atomic age with a visual image that has<br>become imprinted on the human consciousness as a symbol of<br>power and awesome destruction.<br>[![The first 0.11 seconds of the nuclear age, Trinity, July 16, 1945.](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/Trinity4_1.gif)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Resources/photo_gallery/trinity_test.htm) The most common immediate<br>reactions to the explosion were surprise, joy, and<br>relief.  Lawrence was stepping from his car when, in<br>his words, everything went "from darkness to brilliant<br>sunshine in an instant"; he was "momentarily stunned by<br>the surprise." ( [Click here to read Lawrence's thoughts on the Trinity\<br>test.](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/lawrence_trinity.htm)) A military man was heard to exclaim, "The long-hairs<br>have let it get away from them!"  Hans Bethe , who<br>had been looking directly at the explosion, was completely<br>blinded for almost half a minute.  Norris Bradbury<br>reported that "the atom bomb did not fit into any<br>preconceptions possessed by anybody."  The blast wave<br>knocked Kistiakowsky (who was over five miles away) to the<br>ground.  He quickly scrambled to his feet and slapped<br>Oppenheimer on the back, saying, "Oppie, you owe me ten<br>dollars." The physicist Victor Weisskopf reported that<br>"our first feeling was one of elation."  The word<br>Isidor Rabi used was "jubilant."  Within minutes,<br>Rabi was passing around a bottle of whiskey.  At base<br>camp, Bush, Conant, and Groves shook hands.  Rabi<br>reported watching Oppenheimer arrive at base camp after<br>the test:<br> <br>> You've seen pictures of Robert's hat. And he came to where<br>>  we were in the headquarters, so to speak.  And his<br>>  walk was like "High Noon" -- I think it's the best I could<br>>  describe it -- this kind of strut.  He'd done it.<br>[![J. Robert Oppenheimer and General Leslie Groves at the Trinity site, September 1945.](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/OppenheimerGrovesFaces1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/oppenheimer_groves_trinity_image.htm) When they met, Groves said to Oppenheimer, "I am proud of<br>you."  Groves's assistant, Thomas Farrell, remarked<br>to his boss that "the war is over," to which Groves<br>replied, "Yes, after we drop two bombs on Japan." ( [Click here to read Groves's observations of the\<br>Trinity test \[pdf\].](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/groves_memo.htm)) Probably the most mundane response of all was Fermi's:<br>he had calculated ahead of time how far the blast wave<br>might displace small pieces of paper released into<br>it.  About 40 seconds after the explosion, Fermi<br>stood, sprinkled his pre-prepared slips of paper into the<br>atomic wind, and estimated from their deflection that the<br>test had released energy equivalent to 10,000 tons of<br>TNT.  The actual result as it was finally calculated<br>\-\- 21,000 tons (21 kilotons) -- was more than twice what<br>Fermi had estimated with this experiment and<br>_four times_ as much as had been predicted by most<br>at Los Alamos.<br>[![Kenneth Bainbridge](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/Bainbridge1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/kenneth_bainbridge_image.htm) Soon shock and euphoria gave way to more sober<br>reflections.  Rabi reported that after the initial<br>euphoria, a chill soon set in on those present.  The<br>test director, Kenneth Bainbridge, called the explosion a<br>"foul and awesome display" and remarked to Oppenheimer,<br>"Now we are all sons of bitches."  Expressions of<br>horror and remorse are especially common in the later<br>writings of those who were present.  Oppenheimer<br>wrote that the experience called to his mind the legend of<br>Prometheus, punished by Zeus for giving man fire, and said<br>also that he thought fleetingly of Alfred Nobel's vain<br>hope that dynamite would end wars.  Most famously,<br>Oppenheimer later recalled that the explosion had reminded<br>him of a line from the Hindu holy text, the<br>_Bhagavad-Gita_: "Now I am become Death, the<br>destroyer of worlds." The terrifying destructive power of<br>atomic weapons and the uses to which they might be put<br>were to haunt many of the<br>[Manhattan Project scientists](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/People/Scientists/scientists.html) for the<br>remainder of their lives.<br> <br>[![Fat Man at Tinian Island, August 1945](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/FatMan1.gif)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/fat_man_image.htm) The success of the Trinity test meant that both types of<br>bombs -- the uranium design, untested but thought to be<br>reliable, and the plutonium design, which had just been<br>tested successfully -- were now available for use in the<br>war against Japan.  Little Boy, the uranium bomb, was<br>dropped first at<br>[Hiroshima](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945/hiroshima.htm) on August 6, while the plutonium<br>weapon, Fat Man, followed three days later at<br>[Nagasaki](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945/nagasaki.htm) on August 9.  Within days,<br>[Japan offered to surrender](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945/surrender.htm).<br> <br>- [The War Enters Its Final Phase,
