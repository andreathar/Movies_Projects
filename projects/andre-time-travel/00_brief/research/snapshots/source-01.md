### What is Julius Caesar best known for?

Julius Caesar was a Roman general and statesman who played a central role in the transformation of the Roman Republic into the Roman Empire. He is best known for his conquest of [Gaul](https://www.britannica.com/place/Gaul-ancient-region-Europe) (58–50 bce), his victory in the civil war of 49–45 bce, and his appointment as dictator (46–44 bce). During his rule, Caesar initiated a series of political and social reforms that helped lay the groundwork for imperial rule. He was also a skilled orator and writer. Caesar was assassinated by a group of nobles in the Senate House on the [Ides of March](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Ides-of-March) in 44 bce.

### How did Julius Caesar die?

Julius Caesar was murdered in the Roman Senate House by a group of nobles on March 15, 44 bce. The assassination plot was led by [Gaius Cassius Longinus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Gaius-Cassius-Longinus-Roman-quaestor) and [Marcus Junius Brutus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Marcus-Junius-Brutus), and it reportedly involved some 60 men. The conspirators claimed that Caesar’s accumulation of power, including his appointment as dictator, threatened the [Roman Republic](https://www.britannica.com/place/Roman-Republic). Caesar was stabbed an estimated 23 times, though a physician’s report claimed that only one wound was fatal. His death marked a turning point in Roman history, contributing to the end of the republic and the rise of the [Roman Empire](https://www.britannica.com/place/Roman-Empire). Caesar’s assassination later inspired the Shakespearean play [_Julius Caesar_](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Julius-Caesar-by-Shakespeare), which popularized the phrase “beware the [Ides of March](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Ides-of-March),” a reference to March 15.

### Did Julius Caesar marry Cleopatra?

Julius Caesar did not marry [Cleopatra](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Cleopatra-queen-of-Egypt), though the couple had a romantic relationship. After defeating his rival [Pompey](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Pompey-the-Great) in Pelusium, Egypt, in 48 bce, Caesar wintered at Alexandria, where he first met Cleopatra. At the time, she had been displaced as coruler and was battling her [younger brother](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ptolemy-XIII-Theos-Philopator) for the throne. The two became lovers, and Caesar helped return Cleopatra to power. Caesar was married to Calpurnia at the time, though the Roman ruler was known for his numerous extramarital affairs. In 47 bce Cleopatra gave birth to [Ptolemy Caesar](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Caesarion) (also called Caesarion, or “little Caesar”).

Caesar returned to Rome in 46 bce, and Cleopatra made at least one state visit, reportedly staying at Caesar’s private villa. She was in Rome when he was assassinated on March 15, 44 bce.

### How did Julius Caesar change the world?

Julius Caesar was a political and military genius who overthrew Rome’s decaying political order and replaced it with a dictatorship. He triumphed in the Roman Civil War but was assassinated by those who believed that he was becoming too powerful.

### How did Julius Caesar come to power?

Julius Caesar crafted an alliance with [Marcus Licinius Crassus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Marcus-Licinius-Crassus) and [Pompey](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Pompey-the-Great) to form the First [Triumvirate](https://www.britannica.com/topic/triumvirate) and challenge the power of the Roman Senate. After Crassus’s death, Caesar led his army into Italy, defeated Pompey, and claimed the title of dictator.

### What was Julius Caesar’s childhood like?

Julius Caesar’s family was old [Roman nobility](https://www.britannica.com/topic/patrician), but they were not rich. His father died when he was 16, but he received significant support from his mother.

**Julius Caesar** (born July 12/13, 100? bce, Rome \[Italy\]—died March 15, 44 bce, Rome) was a celebrated [Roman](https://www.britannica.com/place/ancient-Rome) [general](https://www.britannica.com/topic/general) and statesman, the conqueror of [Gaul](https://www.britannica.com/place/Gaul-ancient-region-Europe) (58–50 bce), victor in the [civil war](https://www.britannica.com/topic/civil-war) of 49–45 bce, and [dictator](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Roman-dictator) (46–44 bce), who was launching a series of political and social reforms when he was [assassinated](https://www.britannica.com/topic/assassination) by a group of nobles in the Senate House on the [Ides of March](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Ides-of-March). He is one of the major figures of [Classical antiquity](https://www.britannica.com/event/Classical-antiquity).

Caesar changed the course of the [history](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history) of the Greco-Roman world decisively and irreversibly. The Greco-Roman society has been extinct for so long that most of the names of its great men mean little to the average, educated modern person. But Caesar’s name, like [Alexander](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Alexander-the-Great)’s, is still on people’s lips throughout the Christian and [Islamic](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Islam) worlds. Even people who know nothing of Caesar as a historic personality are familiar with his [family name](https://www.britannica.com/topic/surname) as a title signifying a ruler who is in some sense uniquely supreme or paramount—the meaning of _Kaiser_ in German, _tsar_ in the [Slavonic languages](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Slavic-languages), and _qayṣar_ in the languages of the [Islamic world](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Islamic-world).

Caesar’s gens (clan) name, Julius (Iulius), is also familiar in the Christian world, for in Caesar’s lifetime the Roman month Quintilis, in which he was born, was renamed “ [July](https://www.britannica.com/topic/July)” in his honor. This name has survived, as has Caesar’s reform of the [calendar](https://www.britannica.com/science/calendar). The old Roman calendar was inaccurate and manipulated for political purposes. Caesar’s calendar, the [Julian calendar](https://www.britannica.com/science/Julian-calendar), is still partially in force in the [Eastern Orthodox Christian](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Eastern-Orthodoxy) countries, and the [Gregorian calendar](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Gregorian-calendar), now in use in the West, is the Julian, slightly corrected by Pope [Gregory XIII](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Gregory-XIII).

## Family background and career

[![Julius Caesar](https://cdn.britannica.com/11/196711-050-FA58D50D/Julius-Caesar-marble-sculpture-Andrea-di-Pietro.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/11/196711-050-FA58D50D/Julius-Caesar-marble-sculpture-Andrea-di-Pietro.jpg)

[_Julius Caesar_](https://cdn.britannica.com/11/196711-050-FA58D50D/Julius-Caesar-marble-sculpture-Andrea-di-Pietro.jpg) _Julius Caesar_, marble sculpture by Andrea di Pietro di Marco Ferrucci, c. 1512–14; in the Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York City.(more)

Caesar’s gens, the Julii, were [patricians](https://www.britannica.com/topic/patrician)—i.e., members of [Rome](https://www.britannica.com/place/Rome)’s original [aristocracy](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/aristocracy), which had coalesced in the 4th century bce with a number of leading [plebeian](https://www.britannica.com/topic/plebeian) (commoner) families to form the nobility that had been the governing class in Rome since then. By Caesar’s time, the number of surviving [patrician](https://www.britannica.com/topic/patrician) gentes was small; and in the gens Julia the Caesares seem to have been the only surviving family. Though some of the most powerful noble families were patrician, patrician blood was no longer a political advantage; it was actually a handicap, since a patrician was debarred from holding the paraconstitutional but powerful office of [tribune](https://www.britannica.com/topic/tribune-Roman-official) of the plebs. The Julii Caesares traced their [lineage](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/lineage) back to the goddess [Venus](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Venus-goddess), but the family was not snobbish or conservative-minded. It was also not rich or influential or even distinguished.

A Roman noble won distinction for himself and his family by securing election to a series of public offices, which culminated in the [consulship](https://www.britannica.com/topic/consul-ancient-Roman-official), with the [censorship](https://www.britannica.com/topic/censor-ancient-Roman-official) possibly to follow. This was a difficult task for even the ablest and most gifted noble unless he was backed by substantial family wealth and influence. Rome’s victory over [Carthage](https://www.britannica.com/place/Carthage-ancient-city-Tunisia) in the [Second Punic War](https://www.britannica.com/event/Second-Punic-War) (218–201 bce) had made Rome the paramount power in the Mediterranean basin; an influential Roman noble family’s clients (that is, [protégés](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/prot%C3%A9g%C3%A9s) who, in return, gave their patrons their political support) might include kings and even whole nations, besides numerous private individuals. The requirements and the costs of a Roman political career in Caesar’s day were high, and the competition was severe; but the potential profits were of enormous magnitude. One of the perquisites of the [praetorship](https://www.britannica.com/topic/praetor) and the consulship was the government of a [province](https://www.britannica.com/topic/province-ancient-Roman-government), which gave ample opportunity for plunder. The whole Mediterranean world was, in fact, at the mercy of the Roman nobility and of a new class of Roman businessmen, the [equites](https://www.britannica.com/topic/eques) (“knights”), which had grown rich on [military](https://www.britannica.com/topic/armed-force) contracts and on tax farming.

Military manpower was supplied by the Roman peasantry. This class had been partly dispossessed by an economic revolution following on the devastation caused by the Second Punic War. The Roman governing class had consequently come to be hated and discredited at home and abroad. From 133 bce onward there had been a series of alternate revolutionary and counter-revolutionary [paroxysms](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/paroxysms). It was evident that the misgovernment of the Roman state and the Greco-Roman world by the Roman nobility could not continue indefinitely and it was fairly clear that the most probable [alternative](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/alternative) was some form of military [dictatorship](https://www.britannica.com/topic/dictatorship) backed by dispossessed Italian peasants who had turned to long-term military service.

The traditional competition among members of the Roman nobility for office and the spoils of office was thus threatening to turn into a desperate race for seizing autocratic power. The Julii Caesares did not seem to be in the running. It was true that Sextus Caesar, who was perhaps the dictator’s uncle, had been one of the consuls for 91 bce; and [Lucius Caesar](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lucius-Julius-Caesar), one of the consuls for 90 bce, was a distant cousin, whose son and namesake was [consul](https://www.britannica.com/topic/consul-ancient-Roman-official) for 64 bce. In 90 bce, Rome’s Italian allies had seceded from Rome because of the Roman government’s obstinate refusal to grant them Roman citizenship, and, as consul, Lucius Caesar had introduced emergency legislation for granting citizenship to the citizens of all Italian ally states that had not taken up arms or that had returned to their [allegiance](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/allegiance).

Whoever had been consul in this critical year would have had to initiate such legislation, whatever his personal political [predilections](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/predilections). There is evidence, however, that the Julii Caesares, though patricians, had already committed themselves to the antinobility party. An aunt of the future dictator had married [Gaius Marius](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Gaius-Marius), a self-made man ( _novus homo_) who had forced his way up to the summit by his military ability and had made the momentous [innovation](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/innovation) of recruiting his armies from the dispossessed peasants.

The date of Caesar the dictator’s birth has long been disputed. The day was July 12 or 13; the traditional (and perhaps most probable) year is 100 bce; but if this date is correct, Caesar must have held each of his offices two years in advance of the legal minimum age. His father, [Gaius Caesar](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Gaius-Caesar), died when Caesar was but 16; his mother, Aurelia, was a notable woman, and it seems certain that he owed much to her.

In spite of the inadequacy of his resources, Caesar seems to have chosen a political career as a matter of course. From the beginning, he probably privately aimed at winning office, not just for the sake of the honors but in order to achieve the power to put the misgoverned Roman state and Greco-Roman world into better order in accordance with ideas of his own. It is improbable that Caesar deliberately sought [monarchical](https://www.britannica.com/topic/monarchy) power until after he had crossed the [Rubicon](https://www.britannica.com/place/Rubicon) in 49 bce, though [sufficient](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/sufficient) power to impose his will, as he was determined to do, proved to mean monarchical power.

In 84 bce Caesar committed himself publicly to the radical side by marrying Cornelia, a daughter of [Lucius Cornelius Cinna](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lucius-Cornelius-Cinna), a noble who was Marius’s associate in revolution. In 83 bce [Lucius Cornelius Sulla](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Sulla) returned to [Italy](https://www.britannica.com/place/Italy) from the East and led the successful counter-revolution of 83–82 bce; Sulla then ordered Caesar to divorce Cornelia. Caesar refused and came close to losing not only his property (such as it was) but his life as well. He found it advisable to remove himself from Italy and to do military service, first in the province of [Asia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Asia-ancient-Roman-province) and then in [Cilicia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cilicia).

In 78 bce, after Sulla’s death, he returned to Rome and started on his political career in the conventional way, by acting as a prosecuting advocate—of course, in his case, against prominent Sullan counter-revolutionaries. His first target, Gnaeus Cornelius Dolabella, was defended by [Quintus Hortensius](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Quintus-Hortensius), the leading advocate of the day, and was acquitted by the extortion-court jury, composed exclusively of senators.

Caesar then went to [Rhodes](https://www.britannica.com/place/Rhodes-island-Greece) to study oratory under a famous professor, Molon. En route he was captured by pirates (one of the symptoms of the [anarchy](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/anarchy) into which the Roman nobility had allowed the Mediterranean world to fall). Caesar raised his ransom, raised a [naval force](https://www.britannica.com/topic/navy), captured his captors, and had them crucified—all this as a private individual holding no public office. In 74 bce, when [Mithradates VI Eupator](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Mithradates-VI-Eupator), king of [Pontus](https://www.britannica.com/place/Pontus-ancient-district-Turkey), renewed [war](https://www.britannica.com/topic/war) on the Romans, Caesar raised a private army to combat him.

In his absence from Rome, Caesar was made a member of the politico-ecclesiastical college of [pontifices](https://www.britannica.com/topic/pontifex); and on his return he gained one of the elective military tribuneships. Caesar now worked to undo the Sullan constitution in cooperation with [Pompey](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Pompey-the-Great) (Gnaeus Pompeius), who had started his career as a lieutenant of Sulla but had changed sides since Sulla’s death. In 69 or 68 bce Caesar was elected [quaestor](https://www.britannica.com/topic/quaestor-ancient-Roman-official) (the first rung on the Roman political ladder). In the same year his wife, Cornelia, and his aunt Julia, Marius’s widow, died. In public funeral orations in their honor, Caesar found opportunities for praising Cinna and Marius. Caesar afterward married Pompeia, a distant relative of Pompey. Caesar served his quaestorship in the province of Farther Spain (modern [Andalusia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Andalusia-region-Spain) and [Portugal](https://www.britannica.com/place/Portugal)).

Caesar was elected one of the curule [aediles](https://www.britannica.com/topic/aedile) for 65 bce, and he celebrated his [tenure](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/tenure) of this office by unusually lavish expenditure with borrowed money. He was elected pontifex maximus in 63 bce by a political dodge. By now he had become a controversial political figure. After the suppression of [Catiline’s](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Catiline-Roman-politician) [conspiracy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/conspiracy) in 63 bce, Caesar, as well as the millionaire [Marcus Licinius Crassus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Marcus-Licinius-Crassus), was accused of complicity. It seems unlikely that either of them had committed himself to Catiline; but Caesar proposed in the [Senate](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Senate-Roman-history) a more merciful alternative to the [death penalty](https://www.britannica.com/topic/capital-punishment), which the consul [Cicero](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Cicero) was asking for the arrested conspirators. In the uproar in the Senate, Caesar’s motion was defeated.

Caesar was elected a [praetor](https://www.britannica.com/topic/praetor) for 62 bce. Toward the end of the year of his praetorship, a scandal was caused by [Publius Clodius](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Publius-Clodius-Pulcher) in Caesar’s house at the celebration there of the [rites](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/rites), for women only, of [Bona Dea](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Bona-Dea) (a Roman deity of fruitfulness, both in the Earth and in women). Caesar consequently divorced Pompeia. He obtained the governorship of Farther Spain for 61–60 bce. His creditors did not let him leave Rome until Crassus had gone bail for a quarter of his debts; but a military expedition beyond the northwest frontier of his province enabled Caesar to win loot for himself as well as for his soldiers, with a balance left over for the treasury. This partial financial recovery enabled him, after his return to Rome in 60 bce, to stand for the consulship for 59 bce.

# The first triumvirate and the conquest of Gaul

[![Marble statue of Julius Caesar in a toga, standing with one arm extended, displayed indoors with ornate gold-framed decor in the background.](https://cdn.britannica.com/16/146716-050-04657984/statue-Julius-Caesar.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/16/146716-050-04657984/statue-Julius-Caesar.jpg)

[Julius Caesar](https://cdn.britannica.com/16/146716-050-04657984/statue-Julius-Caesar.jpg) Marble statue of Julius Caesar.(more)

The [value](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/value) of the consulship lay in the lucrative provincial governorship to which it would normally lead. On the eve of the consular elections for 59 bce, the Senate sought to allot to the two future consuls for 59 bce, as their proconsular provinces, the unprofitable supervision of forests and cattle trails in [Italy](https://www.britannica.com/place/Italy). The Senate also secured by massive bribery the election of an anti-Caesarean, [Marcus Calpurnius Bibulus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Marcus-Calpurnius-Bibulus). But they failed to prevent Caesar’s election as the other [consul](https://www.britannica.com/topic/consul-ancient-Roman-official).

Caesar now succeeded in organizing an irresistible coalition of political bosses. Pompey had carried out his mission to put the East in order with notable success, but after his return to Italy and his disbandment of his army in 62 bce, the Senate had thwarted him—particularly by preventing him from securing land allotments for his veterans. Caesar, who had assiduously [cultivated](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/cultivated) Pompey’s friendship, now entered into a secret pact with him. Caesar’s master stroke was to persuade Crassus to join the partnership, the so-called first [triumvirate](https://www.britannica.com/topic/triumvirate). Crassus—like Pompey, a former lieutenant of Sulla—had been one of the most active of Pompey’s obstructors so far. Only Caesar, on good terms with both, was in a position to [reconcile](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/reconcile) them. Early in 59 bce, Pompey sealed his alliance with Caesar by marrying Caesar’s only child, [Julia](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Julia-daughter-of-Julius-Caesar). Caesar married Calpurnia, daughter of Lucius Piso, who became consul in 58 bce.

As consul, Caesar introduced a bill for the allotment of Roman public lands in Italy, on which the first charge was to be a provision for Pompey’s soldiers. The bill was vetoed by three tribunes of the [plebs](https://www.britannica.com/topic/plebeian), and Caesar’s colleague [Bibulus](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Marcus-Calpurnius-Bibulus) announced his intention of preventing the transaction of public business by watching the skies for portents whenever the public assembly was [convened](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/convened). Caesar then cowed the opposition by employing some of Pompey’s veterans to make a riot, and the distribution was carried out. Pompey’s settlement of the East was ratified _en bloc_ by an act negotiated by an agent of Caesar, the [tribune](https://www.britannica.com/topic/tribune-Roman-official) of the plebs Publius Vatinius. Caesar himself initiated a noncontroversial and much-needed act for punishing misconduct by governors of provinces.

Another act negotiated by Vatinius gave Caesar Cisalpine [Gaul](https://www.britannica.com/place/Gaul-ancient-region-Europe) (between the Alps, the Apennines, and the Adriatic) and Illyricum. His [tenure](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/tenure) was to last until February 28, 54 bce. When the governor-designate of [Transalpine Gaul](https://www.britannica.com/place/Transalpine-Gaul) suddenly died, this [province](https://www.britannica.com/topic/province-ancient-Roman-government), also, was assigned to Caesar at Pompey’s instance. [Cisalpine Gaul](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cisalpine-Gaul) gave Caesar a [military](https://www.britannica.com/topic/armed-force) recruiting ground; Transalpine Gaul gave him a springboard for conquests beyond [Rome’s](https://www.britannica.com/place/Rome) northwest frontier.

Between 58 and 50 bce, Caesar conquered the rest of [Gaul](https://www.britannica.com/event/Gallic-Wars) up to the left bank of the [Rhine](https://www.britannica.com/place/Rhine-River) and subjugated it so effectively that it remained passive under Roman rule throughout the Roman civil wars between 49 and 31 bce. This achievement was all the more amazing in light of the fact that the Romans did not possess any great superiority in military equipment over the north European barbarians. Indeed, the Gallic cavalry was probably superior to the Roman, horseman for horseman. Rome’s military superiority lay in its mastery of strategy, tactics, [discipline](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/discipline), and [military engineering](https://www.britannica.com/technology/military-engineering). In Gaul, Rome also had the advantage of being able to deal separately with dozens of relatively small, independent, and uncooperative states. Caesar conquered these piecemeal, and the concerted attempt made by a number of them in 52 bce to shake off the Roman yoke came too late.

Great though this achievement was, its relative importance in Caesar’s career and in Roman [history](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history) has been overestimated in Western tradition (as have his brief raids on Britain). In Caesar’s mind his [conquest](https://www.britannica.com/topic/conquest-international-law) of Gaul was probably carried out only as a means to his ultimate end. He was acquiring the military manpower, the plunder, and the [prestige](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/prestige) that he needed to secure a free hand for the prosecution of the task of reorganizing the Roman state and the rest of the Greco-Roman world. This final achievement of Caesar’s looms much larger than his conquest of Gaul, when it is viewed in the wider setting of [world history](https://www.britannica.com/topic/world-history) and not just in the narrower setting of the Greco-Roman civilization’s present daughter civilization in the West.

In 58 bce Rome’s northwestern frontier, established in 125 bce, ran from the [Alps](https://www.britannica.com/place/Alps) down the left bank of the upper [Rhône River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Rhone-River) to the [Pyrenees](https://www.britannica.com/place/Pyrenees), skirting the southeastern foot of the [Cévennes](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cevennes) and including the upper [basin](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/basin) of the [Garonne River](https://www.britannica.com/place/Garonne-River) without reaching the Gallic shore of the Atlantic. In 58 bce Caesar intervened beyond this line, first to drive back the [Helvetii](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Helvetii), who had been migrating westward from their home in what is now central Switzerland. He then crushed Ariovistus, a German soldier of fortune from beyond the Rhine. In 57 bce Caesar subdued the distant and warlike [Belgic](https://www.britannica.com/place/Belgica-ancient-province-Europe) group of Gallic peoples in the north, while his lieutenant Publius Licinius Crassus subdued what are now the regions of [Normandy](https://www.britannica.com/place/Normandy) and [Brittany](https://www.britannica.com/place/Brittany-region-France).

In 56 bce the [Veneti](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Veneti-Italian-people), in what is now southern Brittany, started a [revolt](https://www.britannica.com/topic/insurrection-politics) in the northwest that was supported by the still unconquered [Morini](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Morini) on the Gallic coast of the [Strait of Dover](https://www.britannica.com/place/Strait-of-Dover) and the [Menapii](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Menapii) along the south bank of the lower [Rhine](https://www.britannica.com/place/Rhine-River). Caesar reconquered the Veneti with some difficulty and treated them barbarously. He could not finish off the [conquest](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/conquest) of the Morini and Menapii before the end of the campaigning season of 56 bce; and in the winter of 56–55 bce the Menapii were temporarily expelled from their home by two immigrant [German](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Germanic-peoples) peoples, the Usipetes and Tencteri. These peoples were exterminated by Caesar in 55 bce. In the same year he bridged the Rhine just below [Koblenz](https://www.britannica.com/place/Koblenz-Germany) to raid [Germany](https://www.britannica.com/place/Germany) on the other side of the river, and then crossed the Channel to raid Britain. In 54 bce he raided Britain again and subdued a serious revolt in northeastern Gaul. In 53 bce he subdued further revolts in Gaul and bridged the Rhine again for a second raid.

The crisis of Caesar’s [Gallic war](https://www.britannica.com/event/Gallic-Wars) came in 52 bce. The peoples of central Gaul found a national leader in the [Arvernian](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Arverni) [Vercingetorix](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vercingetorix). They planned to cut off the Roman forces from Caesar, who had been wintering on the other side of the [Alps](https://www.britannica.com/place/Alps). They even attempted to invade the western end of the old Roman province of Gallia Transalpina. Vercingetorix wanted to avoid pitched battles and sieges and to defeat the Romans by cutting off their supplies—partly by cavalry operations and partly by “scorched earth”—but he could not persuade his countrymen to adopt this painful policy wholeheartedly.

The [Bituriges](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Bituriges) insisted on standing siege in their town Avaricum (Bourges), and Vercingetorix was unable to save it from being taken by storm within one month. Caesar then besieged Vercingetorix in Gergovia near modern Clermont-Ferrand. A Roman attempt to storm Gergovia was repulsed and resulted in heavy Roman losses—the first outright defeat that Caesar had suffered in Gaul. Caesar then defeated an attack on the Roman army on the march and was thus able to besiege Vercingetorix in [Alesia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Alesia), to the northwest of [Dijon](https://www.britannica.com/place/Dijon). Alesia, like Gergovia, was a position of great natural strength, and a large Gallic army came to relieve it; but this army was repulsed and dispersed by Caesar, and Vercingetorix then [capitulated](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/capitulated).

During the winter of 52–51 bce and the campaigning season of 51 bce, Caesar crushed a number of sporadic further revolts. The most determined of these rebels were the [Bellovaci](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Bellovaci), between the Rivers [Seine](https://www.britannica.com/place/Seine-River) and [Somme](https://www.britannica.com/place/Somme-River), around [Beauvais](https://www.britannica.com/place/Beauvais). Another rebel force stood siege in the south in the natural fortress of Uxellodunum (perhaps the Puy d’Issolu on the [Dordogne](https://www.britannica.com/place/Dordogne-River)) until its [water supply](https://www.britannica.com/science/water-supply) gave out. Caesar had the survivors’ hands cut off. He spent the year 50 bce in organizing the newly conquered territory. After that, he was ready to settle his accounts with his opponents at home.
