- What is the Napoleonic Code?







The [Napoleonic Code](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Napoleonic-Code/Introduction#ref1) is the French civil code enacted on March 21, 1804, and is still extant, with revisions. It was a major influence on 19th-century civil codes in continental Europe and Latin America.



It consolidated principles established in 1789, including civil equality, equality before the law, the abolition of feudalism, modern contractual forms of property, and the secularization of civil relations. The code strengthened property rights and patriarchal authority.



Originally introduced in areas under French control such as Belgium, Luxembourg, and parts of western Germany, it was later introduced into territories conquered by Napoleon.

- How did the Napoleonic Code change the laws in France?







The Napoleonic Code, enacted in 1804, significantly altered French laws by consolidating principles established in 1789, such as [civil equality](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-France/The-French-Revolution-and-Napoleon-1789-1815#ref362027) and equality before the law, and the [abolition of feudalism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-France/The-French-Revolution-and-Napoleon-1789-1815#ref362027). It also protected individual liberty, freedom of work, freedom of conscience, and the [secular nature of the state](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Napoleon-I/The-Directory#ref16217).



However, it also rolled back some [gains women had made during the Revolution](https://www.britannica.com/topic/history-of-France/The-French-Revolution-and-Napoleon-1789-1815#ref362027), reverting to patriarchal standards that strengthened the rights of the husband and father. The code also reimposed state regulation on occupational groups and strengthened ties to religion, family, and state authority. The [Napoleonic Code](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Napoleonic-Code/Introduction#ref1) has been revised but is still extant and was the main influence on the 19th-century civil codes of most countries of continental Europe and Latin America.

## Forces behind codification

[![The True Story of Napoleon Bonaparte](https://cdn.britannica.com/28/247228-138-D7EB7659/how-did-Napoleon-become-a-legend.jpg?w=800&h=450&c=crop)](https://www.britannica.com/video/how-did-Napoleon-become-a-legend/-287948)

[See all videos for this article](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Napoleonic-Code/images-videos)

The demand for [codification](https://www.britannica.com/topic/law-code) and, indeed, codification itself preceded the Napoleonic era (1799–1815). [Diversity](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/Diversity) of laws was the dominant characteristic of the prerevolutionary legal order. [Roman law](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Roman-law) governed in the south of [France](https://www.britannica.com/place/France), whereas in the northern provinces, including [Paris](https://www.britannica.com/place/Paris), a customary [law](https://www.britannica.com/topic/law) had developed, based largely on feudal Frankish and Germanic institutions. [Marriage](https://www.britannica.com/topic/marriage) and family life were almost exclusively within the control of the [Roman Catholic Church](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Roman-Catholicism) and governed by [canon law](https://www.britannica.com/topic/canon-law). In addition, starting in the 16th century, a growing number of matters were governed by royal decrees and ordinances as well as by a case law developed by the _parlements_. The situation inspired [Voltaire](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Voltaire) to observe that a traveler in France “changes his law almost as often as he changes his horses.” Each area had its own collection of customs, and, despite efforts in the 16th and 17th centuries to organize and codify each of those local customary laws, there had been little success at national unification. Vested interests blocked efforts at codification, because reform would [encroach](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/encroach) upon their privileges.

After the [French Revolution](https://www.britannica.com/event/French-Revolution), codification became not only possible but almost necessary. Powerful groups such as the [manors](https://www.britannica.com/topic/manorialism) and the [guilds](https://www.britannica.com/topic/guild-trade-association) had been destroyed; the [secular](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/secular) power of the church had been suppressed; and the provinces had been transformed into subdivisions of the new national state. Political unification was paired with a growing national [consciousness](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/consciousness), which, in turn, demanded a new body of law that would be uniform for the entire state. The Napoleonic Code, therefore, was founded on the [premise](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/premise) that, for the first time in history, a purely rational law should be created, free from all past [prejudices](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/prejudices) and deriving its content from “sublimated common sense”; its [moral](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/moral) justification was to be found not in ancient custom or monarchical paternalism but in its conformity to the dictates of reason.

Giving expression to those beliefs and to the needs of the revolutionary government, the [National Assembly](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Assembly-historical-French-parliament) adopted a unanimous resolution on September 4, 1791, providing that “there shall be a code of civil laws common for the entire realm.” Further steps toward the actual drafting of a civil code, however, were first taken by the [National Convention](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Convention) in 1793, which established a special commission headed by [Jean-Jacques-Régis de Cambacérès, duke de Parme](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jean-Jacques-Regis-de-Cambaceres-duc-de-Parme), and charged it with the task of completing the project within a month. That [commission](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/commission) prepared within six weeks of its creation a draft code consisting of 719 articles. Though truly revolutionary in both intent and content, the draft was rejected by the convention on the grounds that it was too technical and detailed to be easily understood by all citizens. A second, much-shorter, draft of 297 articles was offered in 1794, but it was little debated and had no success. Cambacérès’s persistent efforts produced a third draft (1796), containing 500 articles, but it was equally ill-fated. Another commission, established in 1799, presented a fourth scheme prepared in part by Jean-Ignace Jacqueminot.

[Napoleon I](https://cdn.britannica.com/93/115193-050-6FFE7E0F/First-Consul-Bonaparte-canvas-Antoine-Jean-Gros-Legion-1802.jpg) _First Consul Bonaparte_, oil on canvas by Antoine-Jean Gros, c. 1802; National Museum of the Legion of Honor, Paris.(more)

Finally, the [consulate](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Consulate-French-history), with [Napoleon Bonaparte](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Napoleon-I) as first consul, resumed the legislative work, and a new commission was nominated. A final draft was submitted first to the legislative section and then to the [plenary](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/plenary) assembly of the newly reorganized [Conseil d’État](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Conseil-dEtat) (“Council of State”). There it was extensively discussed, and with the steadfast participation and vigorous support of Napoleon as chairman, it was enacted into law piecemeal, in the form of 36 statutes passed between 1801 and 1803. On March 21, 1804, those statutes were consolidated in a single body of law—the Code Civil des Français. That title was changed to Code Napoléon in 1807 to honour the emperor who, as first consul of the republic, had brought to completion the monumental legislative undertaking. With the fall of the Napoleonic regime, the original title was restored in 1816. Reference to Napoleon was reinstated in the title of the code in 1852 by a decree of [Louis-Napoléon](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Napoleon-III-emperor-of-France) (later Napoleon III), then president of the [Second Republic](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Second-Republic-French-history). Since September 4, 1870, however, statutes have referred to it simply as the “civil code.”

## Contents of the Napoleonic Code

Under the code all male citizens are equal: [primogeniture](https://www.britannica.com/topic/primogeniture), hereditary nobility, and class privileges are extinguished; civilian institutions are emancipated from [ecclesiastical](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/ecclesiastical) control; freedom of person, freedom of contract, and inviolability of private property are fundamental principles.

The first book of the code deals with the law of persons: the enjoyment of [civil rights](https://www.britannica.com/topic/civil-rights), the protection of personality, [domicile](https://www.britannica.com/topic/domicile), [guardianship](https://www.britannica.com/topic/guardian), tutorship, relations of parents and children, marriage, personal relations of spouses, and the dissolution of marriage by [annulment](https://www.britannica.com/topic/annulment) or [divorce](https://www.britannica.com/topic/divorce). The code subordinated women to their fathers and husbands, who controlled all family property, determined the fate of children, and were favoured in divorce proceedings. Many of those provisions were reformed only in the second half of the 20th century. The second book deals with the law of things: the regulation of property rights— [ownership](https://www.britannica.com/money/ownership), [usufruct](https://www.britannica.com/topic/usufruct), and [servitudes](https://www.britannica.com/topic/servitude-property-law). The third book deals with the methods of [acquiring](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/acquiring) rights: by succession, donation, marriage settlement, and obligations. In the last chapters, the code regulates a number of nominate contracts, legal and conventional mortgages, limitations of actions, and prescriptions of rights.

With regard to obligations, the law establishes the traditional Roman-law categories of [contract](https://www.britannica.com/money/contract-law), quasi-contract, delict, and quasi-delict. Freedom to contract is not spelled out explicitly but is an underlying principle in many provisions.

## Dissemination of the Napoleonic Code and its influence

[![Painting by Jean-Baptiste Mauzaisse showing Napoleon Bonaparte seated and writing the Civil Code, while the winged figure of Time crowns him with a laurel wreath.](https://cdn.britannica.com/68/251868-050-BAF05259/Napoleon-Allegory-civil-code-Jean-Baptiste-Mauzaisse.jpg?w=300)](https://cdn.britannica.com/68/251868-050-BAF05259/Napoleon-Allegory-civil-code-Jean-Baptiste-Mauzaisse.jpg)

[Jean-Baptiste Mauzaisse: _Napoleon, Allegory_](https://cdn.britannica.com/68/251868-050-BAF05259/Napoleon-Allegory-civil-code-Jean-Baptiste-Mauzaisse.jpg) _Napoleon, Allegory_, oil on canvas by Jean-Baptiste Mauzaisse, 1833; in the National Museum of Malmaison and Bois-Préau, France. The painting depicts the French emperor writing the Napoleonic Code.(more)

The code was originally introduced into areas under French control in 1804: [Belgium](https://www.britannica.com/place/Belgium), [Luxembourg](https://www.britannica.com/place/Luxembourg), parts of western [Germany](https://www.britannica.com/place/Germany), northwestern [Italy](https://www.britannica.com/place/Italy), [Geneva](https://www.britannica.com/place/Geneva-Switzerland), and [Monaco](https://www.britannica.com/place/Monaco). It was later introduced into territories conquered by Napoleon: Italy, the [Netherlands](https://www.britannica.com/place/Netherlands), the [Hanseatic](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Hanseatic-League) lands, and much of the remainder of western Germany and [Switzerland](https://www.britannica.com/place/Switzerland). The code is still in use in Belgium, Luxembourg, and Monaco.

During the 19th century, the Napoleonic Code was voluntarily adopted in a number of European and Latin American countries, either in the form of simple translation or with considerable modifications. The Italian Civil Code of 1865, [enacted](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/enacted) after the [unification of Italy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/unification-of-Italy), had a close but indirect relationship with the Napoleonic Code. The new Italian code of 1942 departed to a large extent from that tradition. In the early 19th century, the code was introduced into [Haiti](https://www.britannica.com/place/Haiti) and the [Dominican Republic](https://www.britannica.com/place/Dominican-Republic), and it is still in force there. [Bolivia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Bolivia) and [Chile](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chile) followed closely the arrangement of the code and borrowed much of its substance. The Chilean code was in turn copied by [Ecuador](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ecuador) and [Colombia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Colombia), closely followed by [Uruguay](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uruguay) and [Argentina](https://www.britannica.com/place/Argentina). In [Louisiana](https://www.britannica.com/place/Louisiana-state), the only civil-law state in the [United States](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States) (which is otherwise bound by [common law](https://www.britannica.com/topic/common-law)), the civil code of 1825 (revised in 1870 and still in force) is closely connected with the Napoleonic Code.

The influence of the Napoleonic Code was [diminished](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/diminished) at the turn of the century by the introduction of the [German Civil Code](https://www.britannica.com/topic/German-Civil-Code) (1900) and the [Swiss Civil Code](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Swiss-Civil-Code) (1912); the former was adopted by [Japan](https://www.britannica.com/place/Japan) and the latter by [Turkey](https://www.britannica.com/place/Turkey). In the 20th century, codes in [Brazil](https://www.britannica.com/place/Brazil), [Mexico](https://www.britannica.com/place/Mexico), [Greece](https://www.britannica.com/place/Greece), and [Peru](https://www.britannica.com/place/Peru) were products of a comparative method, with ideas borrowed from the German, French, and Swiss traditions.

More than two centuries after its promulgation, the Napoleonic Code is still living law in a great part of the world. History has thus partly justified the melancholic words uttered by Napoleon in exile: “My real glory is not the forty battles I won, for [Waterloo](https://www.britannica.com/event/Battle-of-Waterloo)’s defeat will destroy the memory of as many victories.…What nothing will destroy, what will live forever, is my Civil Code.”

### What was the French Revolution?

The French Revolution was a period of major social upheaval that began in 1787 and ended in 1799. It sought to completely change the relationship between the rulers and those they governed and to redefine the nature of political power. It proceeded in a back-and-forth process between revolutionary and reactionary forces.

### Why did the French Revolution happen?

There were many reasons. The [bourgeoisie](https://www.britannica.com/topic/bourgeoisie)—merchants, manufacturers, professionals—had gained financial power but were excluded from political power. Those who were socially beneath them had very few rights, and most were also increasingly impoverished. The [monarchy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/monarchy) was no longer viewed as divinely ordained. When the king sought to increase the tax burden on the poor and expand it to classes that had previously been exempt, revolution became all but inevitable.

### Why did the French Revolution lead to war with other nations?

[King Louis XVI](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Louis-XVI) of France yielded to the idea of a new [constitution](https://www.britannica.com/topic/constitution-politics-and-law) and to the sovereignty of the people but at the same time sent emissaries to the rulers of neighboring countries seeking their help in restoring his power. Many revolutionaries, especially the [Girondins](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Girondin), believed that the revolution needed to spread throughout Europe to succeed. An Austro-Prussian army invaded France, and French revolutionary forces pushed outward.

### How did the French Revolution succeed?

In some respects, the French Revolution did not succeed. But the ideas of [representational](https://www.britannica.com/topic/representation-government) democracy and basic property rights took hold, and it sowed the seeds of the later revolutions of [1830](https://www.britannica.com/event/Revolutions-of-1830) and [1848](https://www.britannica.com/event/Revolutions-of-1848).

**French Revolution**, [revolutionary](https://www.britannica.com/topic/revolution-politics) movement that shook [France](https://www.britannica.com/place/France) between 1787 and 1799. It reached its first climax in 1789 when the [ancien régime](https://www.britannica.com/event/ancien-regime) in France was overthrown. The [revolution](https://www.britannica.com/topic/revolution-politics) was a complex series of events in its causes and later in its effects. Its ideas and values—which were associated with such modern [trends](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/trends) as the growth of [liberal democracy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/liberal-democracy), the development of [nationalism](https://www.britannica.com/topic/nationalism), and the rise of socialism—have had worldwide influence.

## Origins of the Revolution

The French Revolution had general causes common to all the revolutions of the West at the end of the 18th century and particular causes that explain why it was by far the most violent and the most universally significant of these revolutions. The first of the general causes was the social structure of the West. The [feudal](https://www.britannica.com/topic/feudalism) regime had been in steady decline since the Middle Ages and had already disappeared in parts of [Europe](https://www.britannica.com/place/Europe). The increasingly numerous and prosperous elite of wealthy commoners—merchants, manufacturers, and professionals, often called the [bourgeoisie](https://www.britannica.com/topic/bourgeoisie)—aspired to political power in those countries where it did not already possess it. Some of the [peasants](https://www.britannica.com/topic/peasantry), many of whom owned land, had attained an improved standard of living and [education](https://www.britannica.com/topic/education) and wanted to get rid of the last vestiges of feudalism so as to acquire the full rights of landowners and to be free to increase their holdings. About half of the peasants, however, remained poor or landless. Furthermore, from about 1730, higher [standards of living](https://www.britannica.com/money/standard-of-living) had reduced the [mortality](https://www.britannica.com/science/mortality-demography) rate among adults considerably. This, together with other factors, had led to an increase in the population of Europe unprecedented for several centuries: it doubled between 1715 and 1800. For France, which, with 26 million inhabitants in 1789, was the most populated country of Europe, the problem was most [acute](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/acute).

A larger population created a greater demand for food and consumer goods. The discovery of new gold mines in [Brazil](https://www.britannica.com/place/Brazil) had led to a general rise in prices throughout the West from about 1730, indicating a prosperous economic situation. From about 1770 this trend slackened, and economic crises, provoking alarm and even revolt, became frequent. Arguments for social reform began to be advanced. The [philosophes](https://www.britannica.com/topic/philosophe)—intellectuals whose writings inspired these arguments—were certainly influenced by 17th-century theorists such as [René Descartes](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Rene-Descartes), [Benedict de Spinoza](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Benedict-de-Spinoza), and [John Locke](https://www.britannica.com/biography/John-Locke), but they came to very different conclusions about political, social, and economic matters. A revolution seemed necessary to apply the ideas of [Montesquieu](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Montesquieu), [Voltaire](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Voltaire), or [Jean-Jacques Rousseau](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jean-Jacques-Rousseau). This [Enlightenment](https://www.britannica.com/event/Enlightenment-European-history) was spread among the educated classes by the many “societies of thought” that were founded at that time: [Masonic](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Freemasonry) lodges, agricultural societies, and reading rooms.

It is uncertain, however, whether revolution would have come without the added presence of a political crisis. Faced with the heavy expenditure that the wars of the 18th century entailed, the rulers of Europe sought to raise money by taxing the nobles and clergy, who in most countries had hitherto been exempt. To justify this, the rulers likewise [invoked](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/invoked) the arguments of advanced thinkers by adopting the role of “ [enlightened despots](https://www.britannica.com/topic/enlightened-despotism).” This provoked reaction throughout Europe from the privileged bodies, diets, and estates. In [North America](https://www.britannica.com/place/North-America) this backlash contributed to the [American Revolution](https://www.britannica.com/event/American-Revolution), which began with the refusal to pay a tax imposed by the king of Great Britain. Monarchs tried to stop this reaction of the [aristocracy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/aristocracy), and both rulers and the privileged classes sought allies among the nonprivileged bourgeois and the peasants.

Although scholarly debate continues about the exact causes of the Revolution, the following reasons are commonly adduced: (1) the [bourgeoisie](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/bourgeoisie) resented its exclusion from political power and positions of honor; (2) the peasants were acutely aware of their situation and were less and less willing to support the anachronistic and burdensome feudal system; (3) the philosophes had been read more widely in France than anywhere else; (4) French participation in the [American Revolution](https://www.britannica.com/event/American-Revolution) had driven the government to the brink of [bankruptcy](https://www.britannica.com/money/bankruptcy); (5) France was the most populous country in Europe, and crop failures in much of the country in 1788, coming on top of a long period of economic difficulties, [compounded](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/compounded) existing restlessness; and (6) the French [monarchy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/monarchy), no longer seen as [divinely ordained](https://www.britannica.com/topic/divine-right-of-kings), was unable to adapt to the political and societal pressures that were being exerted on it.
