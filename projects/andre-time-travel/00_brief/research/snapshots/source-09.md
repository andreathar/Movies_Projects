Robert Oppenheimer, Enrico Fermi, and Ernest Lawrence](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/OppenheimerFermiLawrence1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/oppenheimer_fermi_lawrence_image.htm) In early May 1945, Secretary of War Henry L. Stimson,<br>with the approval of<br>[President Harry S. Truman](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/People/Administrators/harry-truman.html), formed an<br>Interim Committee of top officials charged with<br>recommending the proper use of atomic weapons in wartime<br>and developing a position for the United States on postwar<br>atomic policy.  Stimson headed the advisory group<br>composed of [Vannevar Bush](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/People/Administrators/vannevar-bush.html),<br>[James Conant](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/People/Administrators/james-conant.html), Karl T. Compton, Under<br>Secretary of the Navy Ralph A. Bard, Assistant Secretary<br>of State William L. Clayton, and future Secretary of State<br>James F. Byrnes. <br>[Robert Oppenheimer](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/People/Administrators/robert-oppenheimer.html),<br>[Enrico Fermi](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/People/Scientists/enrico-fermi.html),<br>[Arthur Compton](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/People/Administrators/arthur-compton.html), and<br>[Ernest Lawrence](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/People/Administrators/ernest-lawrence.html) served as scientific<br>advisors (the Scientific Panel), while General George<br>Marshall represented the military.  The committee met<br>on May 31 and then again the next day with leaders from<br>the business side of the Manhattan Project, including<br>Walter S. Carpenter of DuPont, James C. White of Tennessee<br>Eastman, George H. Bucher of Westinghouse, and James A.<br>Rafferty of Union Carbide.<br>[![President Harry S. Truman, November 1945](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/TrumanPortraitNov45_1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/truman_image.htm) At the May 31 meeting, Lawrence suggested that a<br>demonstration of the atomic bomb might possibly convince<br>the Japanese to surrender.  This was rejected,<br>however, out of fear that the bomb might be a dud, that<br>the Japanese might put American prisoners of war in the<br>area, or that they might manage to shoot down the<br>plane.  The shock value of the new weapon could also<br>be lost. These reasons and others convinced the group that<br>the bomb should be dropped without warning on a "dual<br>target" -- a war plant surrounded by workers'<br>homes.  On June 6, Stimson informed President Truman<br>(right) that the Interim Committee recommended keeping the<br>atomic bomb a secret until Japan had been bombed. <br>The attack should take place as soon as possible and<br>without warning.  Truman and Stimson agreed that the<br>President would stall if the Soviet Union asked about<br>atomic weapons in the upcoming meetings to be held at<br>Potsdam and that it might be possible to gain concessions<br>from the Soviet Union later in return for providing<br>technical information.<br>[![Joe 1, the first Soviet atomic test, August 29, 1949.](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/Joe1SovietTest1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/joe1_image.htm) The Interim Committee also discussed the postwar fate of<br>atomic energy. At the May 31 meeting, they concluded that<br>the United States should try to retain superiority of<br>nuclear weapons in case international relations<br>deteriorated.  Most present at the meeting thought<br>that atomic secrets should be protected for the present,<br>though they conceded that the United States monopoly could<br>not be held long. The meeting with the industrialists<br>confirmed their view that the United States had a lead of<br>three to ten years on the Soviet Union in production<br>facilities for bomb fabrication.  There had been some<br>discussion of free exchange of nuclear research for<br>peaceful purposes and the international inspection system<br>that such an exchange would require.  Stimson told<br>Truman that the Interim Committee was considering domestic<br>legislation and that its members generally held the<br>position that international agreements should be made in<br>which all nuclear research would be made public and a<br>system of inspections would be devised.  In case<br>international agreements were not forthcoming, the United<br>States should continue to produce as much fissionable<br>material as possible to take advantage of its current<br>position of superiority.<br>[![Leo Szilard with Albert Einstein](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/Szilard1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Resources/photo_gallery/events_images.htm) Not all the scientists of the Manhattan Project were<br>satisfied that their voices had been heard in<br>decision-making about the bomb.  They had built the<br>bomb and thought they had a right to help determine how it<br>was to be used. The Scientific Panel of the Interim<br>Committee was supposed to be the connection between the<br>scientists and the policymakers, but after the scientists<br>of the [Met Lab](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Places/MetLab/met-lab.html) were briefed by Arthur<br>Compton on June 2 about the Interim Committee's<br>conclusions, the Met Lab decided to create a "second<br>opinion."  The result was the Committee on the<br>Social and Political Implications of the Atomic Bomb,<br>which was chaired by James Franck and included<br>[Glenn Seaborg](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/People/Scientists/glenn-seaborg.html) and<br>[Leo Szilard](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/People/Scientists/leo-szilard.html).  Its report argued that<br>postwar international control of atomic power was the only<br>way to stop the [arms race](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945-present/cold_war.htm) that would be<br>inevitable if the United States bombed Japan without first<br>demonstrating the weapon in an uninhabited area. <br>Oppenheimer, Fermi, Compton, and Lawrence (the Scientific<br>Panel) disagreed with the Franck Report, however, and<br>concluded that no technical test would convince Japan to<br>surrender.  On June 21, the Interim Committee<br>concurred.  The bomb would be used as soon as<br>possible, without warning, and against a war plant<br>[![Generals Leslie Groves and Thomas Farrell](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/GrovesFarrell1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/groves_farrell_image.htm) surrounded by additional buildings.  As to informing<br>the Soviet Union, the Committee concluded that Truman<br>should mention at Potsdam that the United States was<br>preparing to use a new kind of weapon against Japan.<br> <br>The bomb target selection group was chaired by<br>[Leslie Groves](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/People/Administrators/leslie-groves.html), a responsibility he shared<br>with General Thomas Farrell, his military aide since<br>February 1945.  In late May, the committee of<br>scientists and Army Air Force officers listed Kokura<br>Arsenal, Hiroshima, Niigata, and Kyoto as the four best<br>targets, believing that attacks on these cities would make<br>a profound psychological impression on the Japanese and<br>weaken military resistance.  (None of these cities<br>had yet been bombed by Curtis LeMay's Twentieth Air Force,<br>which planned to eliminate all major Japanese cities by<br>January 1, 1946.)  Stimson vetoed Kyoto, Japan's most<br>cherished cultural center, and Nagasaki replaced Kyoto on<br>the target list.  Now all that was left was for<br>Truman to give his final approval, and then it would be up<br>to the weather to determine which of these four cities<br>would be the first struck by an atomic bomb.<br> <br>- [The War Enters Its Final Phase, 1945](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945/war_1945.htm)<br>-
