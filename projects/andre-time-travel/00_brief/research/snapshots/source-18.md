1939-1945](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945/retrospect.htm)<br>In the early morning hours of August 6, 1945, a B-29<br>bomber named _Enola Gay_ took off from the island<br>of Tinian and headed north by northwest toward<br>Japan.  The bomber's primary target was the<br>[city of Hiroshima](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Places/Other/hiroshima.html), located on the deltas<br>of southwestern Honshu Island facing the Inland Sea. <br>Hiroshima had a civilian population of almost 300,000 and<br>was an important military center, containing about 43,000<br>soldiers.<br> <br>[![Little Boy at Tinian Island, August 1945](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/LittleBoy2.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/little_boy_image.htm) The bomber, piloted by the commander of the 509th<br>Composite Group, Colonel Paul Tibbets, flew at low<br>altitude on automatic pilot before climbing to 31,000 feet<br>as it neared the target area.  At approximately 8:15<br>a.m. Hiroshima time the _Enola Gay_ released<br>"Little Boy," its 9,700-pound<br>[uranium gun-type bomb](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Science/BombDesign/gun-type.html), over the<br>city.  Tibbets immediately dove away to avoid the<br>anticipated shock wave.  Forty-three seconds later, a<br>huge [explosion](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Processes/BombTesting/explosion.html) lit the morning sky as<br>Little Boy detonated 1,900 feet above the city, directly<br>over a parade field where soldiers of the Japanese Second<br>Army were doing calisthenics.  Though already eleven<br>and a half miles away, the _Enola Gay_ was rocked<br>by the blast.  At first, Tibbets thought he was<br>taking flak.  After a second [![Enola Gay returning from Hiroshima mission, Tinian Field, August 6, 1945](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/EnolaGay1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/enola_gay_image.htm)<br>shock wave (reflected from the ground) hit the plane, the<br>crew looked back at Hiroshima.  "The city was<br>hidden by that awful cloud . . . boiling up, mushrooming,<br>terrible and incredibly tall," Tibbets<br>recalled.  The [yield](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Processes/BombTesting/yield.html) of the<br>explosion was later estimated at 15 kilotons (the<br>equivalent of 15,000 tons of TNT).<br> <br>On the ground moments before the blast it was a calm and<br>sunny Monday morning.  An air raid alert from earlier<br>that morning had been called off after only a solitary<br>aircraft was seen (the weather plane), and by 8:15 the<br>city was alive with activity -- soldiers doing their<br>morning calisthenics, commuters on foot or on bicycles,<br>groups of women and children working [![Victim of atomic attack with the pattern of her clothing burned into her back.](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/BurnsBack1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/burns_back_image.htm)<br>outside to clear firebreaks.  Those closest to the<br>explosion died instantly, their bodies turned to black<br>char.  Nearby birds burst into flames in mid-air, and<br>dry, combustible materials such as paper instantly ignited<br>as far away as 6,400 feet from ground zero.  The<br>white light acted as a giant flashbulb, burning the dark<br>patterns of clothing onto skin (right) and the shadows of<br>bodies onto walls.  Survivors outdoors close to the<br>blast generally describe a literally blinding light<br>combined with a sudden and overwhelming wave of<br>[heat](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Processes/BombTesting/thermal-radiation.html). (The effects of<br>[radiation](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Processes/BombTesting/nuclear-radiation.html) are usually not immediately<br>apparent.)  The [blast](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Processes/BombTesting/blast.html) wave followed<br>almost instantly for those close-in, often knocking them<br>from their feet.  Those that were indoors were<br>usually spared the flash burns, but flying glass from<br>broken windows filled most rooms, and all but the very<br>strongest structures collapsed.  One boy was blown<br>through the windows of his house and across the street as<br>the house collapsed behind him.  Within minutes 9 out<br>of 10 people half a mile or less from ground zero were<br>dead.<br> <br>[![Before and After aerial photographs of Hiroshima](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/HiroshimaPostAttack1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Resources/photo_gallery/hiroshima_images.htm) People farther from the point of detonation experienced<br>first the flash and heat, followed seconds later by a<br>deafening boom and the blast wave.  Nearly every<br>structure within one mile of ground zero was destroyed,<br>and almost every building within three miles was<br>damaged.  Less than 10 percent of the buildings in<br>the city survived without any damage, and the blast wave<br>shattered glass in suburbs twelve miles<br>away.  The most common first [![Hiroshima mushroom cloud (picture taken from the ground)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/HiroshimaCloudGround1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/hiroshima_cloud_ground_image.htm)<br>reaction of those that were indoors even miles from ground<br>zero was that their building had just suffered a direct<br>hit by a bomb.  Small ad hoc rescue parties soon<br>began to operate, but roughly half of the city's<br>population was dead or injured.  In those areas most<br>seriously affected virtually no one escaped serious<br>injury.  The numerous small fires that erupted<br>simultaneously all around the city soon merged into one<br>large firestorm, creating extremely strong winds that blew<br>towards the center of the fire.  The firestorm<br>eventually engulfed 4.4 square miles of the city, killing<br>anyone who had not escaped in the first minutes after the<br>attack.  One postwar study of the victims of<br>Hiroshima found that less than 4.5 percent of survivors<br>suffered leg fractures.  Such injuries were not<br>uncommon; it was just that most who could not walk were<br>engulfed by the firestorm.<br> <br>[!["A-bomb Dome" amidst ruins of Hiroshima (the dome is now a World Heritage Site).](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/HiroshimaRuins1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/hiroshima_ruins_image.htm) Even after the flames had subsided, relief from the<br>outside was slow in coming.  For hours after the<br>attack the Japanese government did not even know for sure<br>what had happened.  Radio and telegraph<br>communications with Hiroshima had suddenly ended at 8:16<br>a.m., and vague reports of some sort of large explosion<br>had begun to filter in, but the Japanese high command knew<br>that no large-scale air raid had taken place over the city<br>and that there were no large stores of explosives<br>there.  Eventually a Japanese staff officer was<br>dispatched by plane to survey the city from overhead, and<br>while he was still nearly 100 miles away from the city he<br>began to report on a huge cloud of smoke that hung over<br>it.  The first confirmation of exactly what had<br>happened came only sixteen hours later with the<br>announcement of the bombing by the [![Casualties in a makeshift hospital, Hiroshima](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/HiroshimaHospital1.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/hiroshima_hospital_image.htm)<br>United States.  Relief workers from outside the city<br>eventually began to arrive and the situation stabilized<br>somewhat.  Power in undamaged areas of the city was<br>even restored on August 7th, with limited rail service<br>resuming the following day.  Several days after the<br>blast, however, medical staff began to recognize the first<br>symptoms of [radiation sickness](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Science/Radioactivity/rad-sickness.html) among the<br>survivors.  Soon the death rate actually began to<br>climb again as patients who had appeared to be recovering<br>began suffering from this strange new<br>illness.  Deaths from radiation sickness did not<br>peak until three to four weeks after the attacks and did<br>not taper off until seven to eight weeks after the<br>attack.  Long-range health dangers associated with<br>radiation exposure, such as an increased danger of cancer,<br>would linger for the rest of the victims' lives, as would<br>the psychological effects of the attack.<br> <br>[![Devastation at Hiroshima](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/HiroshimaDevastation2.jpg)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/hiroshima_devastation_image.htm) No one will ever know for certain how many died as a<br>result of the attack on Hiroshima.  Some 70,000<br>people probably died as a result of initial blast, heat,<br>and radiation effects.  This included about twenty<br>American airmen being held as prisoners in the city. <br>By the end of 1945, because of the lingering effects of<br>radioactive [fallout](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Processes/BombTesting/fallout.html) and other after<br>effects, the Hiroshima death toll was probably over<br>100,000.  The five-year death total may have reached<br>or even exceeded 200,000, as cancer and other long-term<br>effects took hold.<br> <br>At 11:00 a.m., August 6 (Washington D.C. time), radio<br>stations began playing a prepared statement from<br>[President Truman](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/People/Administrators/harry-truman.html) informing the American<br>public that the United States had dropped an entirely new<br>type of bomb on the Japanese city of Hiroshima --<br>an "atomic bomb." Truman warned that if<br>Japan still refused to surrender unconditionally, as<br>demanded by the [Potsdam Declaration](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945/potsdam_decision.htm) of<br>July 26, the United States would attack additional targets<br>with equally devastating results.  Two days later, on<br>August 8, the Soviet Union declared war on Japan and<br>attacked Japanese forces in Manchuria, ending American<br>hopes that the war would end before Russian entry into the<br>Pacific<br>[![Fat Man at Tinian Island, August 1945](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/FatMan1.gif)](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/images/fat_man_image.htm) theater.  By August 9th, American aircraft were<br>showering leaflets all over Japan informing its people<br>that "We are in possession of the most destructive<br>explosive ever devised by man.  A single one of our<br>newly developed atomic bombs is actually the equivalent in<br>explosive power to what 2,000 of our giant B-29s can carry<br>on a single mission.  This awful fact is one for you<br>to ponder and we solemnly assure you it is grimly<br>accurate.  We have just begun to use this weapon<br>against your homeland.  If you still have any doubt,<br>make inquiry as to what happened to Hiroshima when just<br>one atomic bomb fell on that city."  Meanwhile,<br>Tibbets's bomber group was simply waiting for the weather<br>to clear in order to drop its next bomb, the<br>[plutonium implosion weapon](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Science/BombDesign/implosion.html) nicknamed<br>"Fat Man" (left) that was destined for the city<br>of [Nagasaki](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Places/Other/nagasaki.html).<br> <br>- [The War Enters Its Final Phase,
