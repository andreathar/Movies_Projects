| ## **The** **Mongol Empire in World History**<br>### **Timothy May**   **North Georgia College and State University** |  |
| **World History and the Mongols**<br>     An empire arose in the steppes of Mongolia <br>in the thirteenth century that forever changed the map of the world, opened <br>intercontinental trade, spawned new nations, changed the course of leadership <br>in two religions, and impacted history indirectly in a myriad of other <br>ways. At its height, the Mongol Empire was the largest contiguous empire <br>in history, stretching from the Sea of Japan to the Carpathian Mountains. <br>Although its impact on Eurasia during the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries <br>was enormous, the Mongol Empire's influence on the rest of the world—particularly <br>its legacy—should not be ignored. <br>**Brief History**<br>     The formation of the Mongol Empire was <br>a slow and arduous process, beginning with the unification of the Mongol <br>and Turkic tribes that dwelt in the Mongolian steppes. Temüjin (1165-1227) <br>emerged on the steppes as a charismatic leader, slowly gaining a following <br>before becoming a _nökhör_ (companion or vassal) to <br>Toghril (d. 1203/1204), Khan of the Kereits, the dominant tribe in central <br>Mongolia. While in the service of Toghril, Temüjin's talents allowed <br>him to become a major leader among the Mongol tribes. Eventually, Temüjin's <br>increase in power and the jealousy it provoked among other members of <br>Toghril's supporters caused Temüjin and Toghril to part ways and <br>ultimately to clash in battle. Their quarrel came to a head in 1203 with <br>Temüjin emerging as the victor. <br>     Temüjin unified the tribes of Mongolia <br>by 1206 into a single supra-tribe known as the _Khamag Mongol Ulus_ <br>or the All Mongol State. In doing so, Temüjin reorganized the social <br>structure by dissolving old tribal lines and regrouping them into an army <br>based on a decimal system (units of 10, 100, and 1000). Furthermore, he <br>instilled a strong sense of discipline into the army. Although he had <br>defeated all of his rivals by 1204, it was not until 1206 that Temüjin's <br>followers recognized him as the sole authority in Mongolia by granting <br>him the title of Chinggis Khan (Genghis Khan), meaning Firm, Fierce, or <br>Resolute Ruler. [1](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn1 "")<br>**Expansion of the Mongol Empire**<br>     Mongol power quickly extended beyond Mongolia, <br>as the Mongols conquered the Tangut kingdom Xixia (modern Ningxia and <br>Gansu provinces of China) by 1209. [2](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn2 "") In 1211 Chinggis Khan <br>invaded the Jin Empire (1125-1234) of Northern China. Although these campaigns <br>began as raids, as their successes increased the Mongols retained the <br>territory they plundered after resistance ceased. Although the Mongols <br>won stunning victories and conquered most of the Jin Empire by 1216, the <br>Jin opposition to the Mongols continued until 1234, seven years after <br>the death of Chinggis Khan. [3](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn3 "")<br>     Mongol expansion into Central Asia began <br>in 1209, as the Mongols pursued tribal leaders who opposed Chinggis Khan's <br>rise to power in Mongolia and thus constituted a threat to his authority <br>there. With their victories, the Mongols gained new territory. Several <br>smaller polities such as the Uighurs of the Tarim Basin also sought the <br>protection of Chinggis Khan as vassals. Ultimately, the Mongols found <br>themselves with a large empire, now bordering not only the Chinese states <br>but also the Islamic world in Central Asia including the Khwarazmian Empire, <br>which spanned over portions of Central Asia, Afghanistan, Iran, and part <br>of modern Iraq. [4](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn4 "")<br>     Initially, Chinggis Khan sought a peaceful <br>commercial relationship with the Khwarazmian state. This abruptly came <br>to an end with the massacre of a Mongol sponsored caravan by the governor <br>of Otrar, a Khwarazmian border town. After diplomatic means failed to <br>resolve the issue, Chinggis Khan left a token force in North China and <br>marched against the Khwarazmians in 1218. [5](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn5 "")<br>     After capturing Otrar, Chinggis Khan divided <br>his army and struck the Khwarazmian Empire at several points. With his <br>more numerous army spread across the empire in an attempt to defend its <br>cities, Muhammad Khwarazmshah II could not compete with the more mobile <br>Mongol army in the field. For the Muslim population, their defeat went <br>beyond simple military conquest; it seemed that God had forsaken them. <br>Indeed, the Mongols cultivated this idea. After capturing Bukhara, Chinggis <br>Khan ascended the pulpit in the Friday mosque and announced:<br>> O people, know that you have committed great sins, and that the great <br>> ones among you have committed these sins. If you ask me what proof I <br>> have for these words, I say it is because I am the punishment of God. <br>> If you had not committed great sins, God would not have sent a punishment <br>> like me upon you. [6](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn6 "")<br>     Meanwhile, Muhammad II watched his cities <br>fall one by one until he fled with a Mongol force in pursuit. He successfully <br>eluded them and escaped to an island in the Caspian Sea, where he died <br>shortly thereafter from dysentery. Although his son, Jalal al-Din (d. <br>1230) attempted to rally the empire in Afghanistan, Chinggis Khan defeated <br>him near the Indus River in 1221, forcing Jalal al-Din to flee to India.<br>     The Khwarazmian Empire was now ripe for <br>annexation but Chinggis Khan kept only the territory north of the Amu <br>Darya, thus not over-extending his army. He then returned to Mongolia <br>in order to deal with a rebellion in Xixia which broke out while the Mongol <br>leader was in Central Asia. [7](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn7 "") <br>After resting his army, he invaded Xixia in 1227 and besieged the capital <br>of Zhongxing. During the course of the siege, Chinggis Khan died from <br>injuries sustained from a fall from his horse while hunting. Yet he ordered <br>his sons and army to continue the war against Xixia. Indeed, even as he <br>lay ill in his bed, Chinggis Khan instructed them, "While I take my meals <br>you must talk about the killing and the destruction of the Tang'ut and <br>say, 'Maimed and tamed, they are no more.'" [8](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn8 "")<br>     The army that Chinggis Khan organized was <br>the key to Mongol expansion. It fought and operated in a fashion that <br>other medieval armies did not, or could not, replicate. [9](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn9 "") <br>In essence it operated very much as a modern army does, over multiple <br>fronts and in several corps but in a coordinated effort. Also, the Mongols <br>fought in the manner of total war. The only result that mattered was the <br>defeat of enemies through any means necessary, including ruses and trickery. <br>The famous traveler, Marco Polo, observed <br>> In truth they are stout and valiant soldiers, and inured to war. And <br>> you perceive that it is just when the enemy sees them run, and imagines <br>> that he gained the battle, that he has in reality lost it, for the \[Mongols\] <br>> wheel round in a moment when they judge the right time has come. And <br>> after his fashion they have won many a fight. [10](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn10 "")<br>**Empire after Chinggis Khan**<br>      Ögödei (d.1240-41), Chinggis <br>Khan's second son, ascended the throne in 1230 and quickly resumed operations <br>against the Jin Empire, successfully conquering it in 1234. Although Chinggis <br>Khan had announced previously that he had been sent as the scourge of <br>God, Ögödei promoted the idea that Heaven ( _Tengri_ the <br>sky god) had declared that the Mongols were destined to rule the world. <br>Before invading a region, Mongol envoys delivered correspondence indicating <br>that as Heaven had decreed that the Mongols were to rule the earth, a <br>prince should come to the Mongol court and offer his submission. Any refusal <br>to this request was seen as an act of rebellion not only against the Mongols, <br>but also against the will of Heaven. This process was aided by a multi-ethnic <br>bureaucracy staffed not only by Mongols, but in fact in large part by <br>the educated elites from the sedentary conquered populations such as Chinese, <br>Persians, and Uighurs. Thus the letters were translated and delivered <br>in triplicate—each one being in another language so that there was <br>a high probability that someone at the other court could read the letter.<br>      Ögödei backed his intentions <br>of world domination by sending armies out to multiple fronts. While Ögödei <br>led his army against the Jin, another army conquered Iran, Armenia, and <br>Georgia under the command of Chormaqan (d.1240). Meanwhile, a massive <br>force under the leadership of Prince Batu (fl. 1227-1255) and Sübedei <br>(1176-1248), the renowned Mongol general, marched west, conquering the <br>Russian principalities and the Pontic and Caspian steppes before invading <br>Hungary and Poland. While they did not seek to control Hungary and Poland, <br>the Mongols left both areas devastated before departing, possibly due <br>to Ögödei's death in 1241. [11](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn11 "")<br>     Ögödei's son, Güyük, <br>came to the throne in 1246 only after a lengthy debate over who would <br>succeed his father. In the interim, Güyük's mother Toregene <br>served as regent. Once in power, Güyük accomplished little in <br>terms of conquest as he died in 1248. His wife, Oghul-Qaimish, served <br>as regent but did little to assist in choosing a new khan. Her inattention <br>led to a coup in which Möngke b. Tolui (d. 1250-51) seized power <br>with the backing of most of the Chinggisid princes in 1250. Under his <br>reign the Mongol armies were once again on the march. He and his brother <br>Qubilai (d. 1295) led armies into the territory of China's Southern Song <br>(1126-1279), south of the Yangtze River, while Hülegü (d. 1265), <br>another brother, led an army into the Middle East. <br>     Hülegü's forces successfully <br>destroyed the Ismailis in 1256, a Shi'a group in northern Iran also known <br>as the Assassins. The Persian chronicler, Juvaini, who also worked in <br>the Mongol bureaucracy, reveled in the destruction of the much feared <br>Ismailis, who used assassination in order to intimidate and extend their <br>influence in parts of the Middle East. Juvaini wrote that "So was the <br>world cleansed which had been polluted by their evil. Wayfarers now ply <br>to and fro without fear or dread or the inconvenience of paying a toll <br>and pray for the fortune of the happy King who uprooted their foundations <br>and left no trace of anyone of them." [12](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn12 "")<br>     Hülegü then moved against the <br>Abbasid Caliphate in Baghdad. The Caliph, nominally the titular leader <br>of Sunni Islam, refused to capitulate but did little to defend the city. <br>The Mongols sacked Baghdad and executed the Caliph, ending the position <br>of Caliph among the Sunnis in 1258. Hülegü's armies invaded <br>Syria, successfully capturing Aleppo and Damascus. Hülegü however, <br>withdrew the bulk of his army in 1259-60 after receiving news that Mongke <br>had died during the war against the Song. Meanwhile, the Mamluk Sultanate <br>of Egypt struck the Mongol garrisons in Syria, defeating them at Ayn Jalut <br>in 1260. As the Mongol Empire spiraled into civil war after the death <br>of Mongke, Hülegü never recovered the Syrian conquests. Instead, <br>civil war with the Mongols in the Pontic and Caspian steppes (the so-called <br>Golden Horde), and those in Central Asia, occupied much of his attention.<br>     Due to the lack of a clear principle of <br>succession other than being descended from Chinggis Khan, warfare between <br>rival claimants was frequent. Civil war erupted after Möngke's death <br>as two of his brothers vied for the throne. Qubilai eventually defeated <br>Ariq Boke in 1265, but the damage to the territorial integrity of the <br>Empire was great. While the other princes nominally accepted Qubilai as <br>the Khan of the empire, his influence dwindled outside of Mongolia and <br>China. Qubilai and his successors, known as the Yuan dynasty (1279-1368), <br>found their closest allies in Hülegü and his successors. Hülegü's <br>kingdom, known as the Il-khanate of Persia, dominated Iran, Iraq, modern <br>Turkey, Armenia, Azerbaijan, and Georgia. Central Asia was ruled by the <br>Chaghatayids, the descendents of Chaghatay, Chinggis Khan's third son, <br>although often they were the puppets of Qaidu, a descendent of Ögödei <br>and rival of Qubilai Khan. Meanwhile in Russia and the Pontic and Caspian <br>steppes, descendents of Jochi, Chinggis Khan's first son, held power. <br>Their state was often referred to as the Golden Horde in later periods.<br>     Since the Mongol Empire was the largest <br>contiguous state in history, its impact on world history is incalculable <br>as it impacted the pre-modern world in a variety of ways, both directly <br>and indirectly. To discuss this impact, one could write a monograph, thus <br>this discussion will be limited to an overview of only three areas: geography, <br>trade, and religion. <br>**Geography**<br>     The Mongol expansion forever changed the <br>face of Asia in terms of both political and human geography, beginning <br>in Mongolia. Originally, the Mongols were but one tribe among several. <br>Under Chinggis Khan, all of the tribes were united into one new collective <br>unit: the _Khamag Mongol Ulus_, or united Mongol nation, which then <br>evolved into the _Yeke Mongol Ulus_ or Great Mongol Nation or state, <br>as the Mongols began to expand their empire. [13](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn13 "") Furthermore, tribal <br>identities were stripped away by disposing of old tribal elites and a <br>new social organization was imposed that focused on the family of Chinggis <br>Khan, or the _altan urugh_. The Mongolian nation of the modern era <br>exists today because of the rise of the Mongol Empire. <br>     This fact is very evident when one visits <br>Mongolia. One flies into Ulaanbaatar, the capital, at Chinggis Khan Airport, <br>drives down Chinggis Khan Avenue, can change money at Chinggis Khan bank <br>and receive tögrögs with Chinggis Khan's face on every bill <br>from one hundred to ten thousand tögrögs. And of course, one <br>might stay at Chinggis Khan Hotel, attend Chinggis Khan University, and <br>imbibe either Chinggis Khan beer or one of the several fine varieties <br>of Chinggis Khan vodka. Whereas under communist rule the great Mongol <br>leader was denigrated as a feudal oppressor, today he is more ubiquitous <br>than Michael Jordan as an advertisement prop in the 1990s. Furthermore, <br>Chinggis Khan is not only the father of the country, but many—including <br>academics and politicians—view Chinggis Khan as the reason why Mongolia <br>has successfully transitioned into a democratic state. In the eyes of <br>many Mongolians, the framework for democracy was created by Chinggis Khan <br>by having his successors elected. [14](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn14 "") One may quibble with this view: in <br>fact, the Mongol khans were chosen only from the descendents of Chinggis <br>Khan. However, what is important is that this idea succors the Mongolian <br>population and helps rationalize a new form of government, thus giving <br>it legitimacy and a quasi-historical foundation. <br>     A more apparent legacy of Chinggis Khan <br>and the Mongol Empire on Mongolia is the creation of a writing system. <br>Although illiterate himself, Chinggis Khan imposed a written language <br>upon the Mongols. Having seen the value of writing among the Naiman, one <br>of the tribes he defeated in 1204, Chinggis Khan ordered that a Mongolian <br>script be instituted. [15](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn15 "") This script was adapted <br>from the Uighur script, itself based on Syriac learned from Nestorian <br>Christian missionaries, and written vertically. [16](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn16 "") It remained in use in modern Mongolia <br>until the twentieth century, when it was replaced with a modified Cyrillic <br>script by the Communist government, but remains the written form of Mongolian <br>today in the Inner Mongolian Autonomous Region of China. Since the fall <br>of communism in Mongolia, there has been discussion of reviving it there. <br>However, seventeen years later it still has not supplanted the Cyrillic.<br>     The Mongol expansion also caused the movement <br>of other tribes, primarily Turkic, setting off large-scale migrations <br>and spreading Turkic culture. Some of this was through the machinations <br>of the Mongol Empire, while other migrations were attempts to avoid the <br>Mongols. While some Turks, such as the Kipchaks of the Pontic and Caspian <br>steppes, moved into Hungary and the Balkans, others, primarily Oghuz Turks, <br>moved into Anatolia or modern day Turkey. A strong Turkic presence existed <br>in Anatolia since the eleventh century, but the new influx of Turks eventually <br>led to the Turkicization of the many areas of the Middle East and Central <br>Asia. <br>     Among those groups that moved into the <br>region was the Osmanli, who established the Ottoman Empire in the fourteenth <br>century. They entered Anatolia after fleeing from what is now Afghanistan <br>during the Mongols invasion of the Khwarazmian Empire. While much debate <br>continues among scholars on the impact of the Mongols on the origins of <br>the Ottoman Empire, there are a few who argue that many of the institutions <br>of the early Ottoman state were based on Mongol practices. [17](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn17 "") This appears as a logical <br>premise since the Mongols dominated Anatolia until the fourteenth century. <br>Indeed, the Osmanli state emerged in the vacuum caused by the collapse <br>of Mongol authority in that region. <br>     Later Turkic nations also emerged from <br>the Mongols, such as the Tatars of Crimea and Kazan. The Tatars were direct <br>offshoots from the collapse of the Golden Horde in the later fifteenth <br>century. Both the Kazakhs and Uzbeks trace their origins to the Golden <br>Horde. The Uzbeks, named after Uzbek Khan, the ruler of the Golden Horde <br>during its Golden Age, also came from the splintering of the Golden Horde. <br>The Kazakhs, in turn, split from the Uzbeks and remained a primarily nomadic <br>people until the twentieth century, whereas the Uzbeks settled in the <br>more urban areas of Central Asia in the sixteenth century. [18](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn18 "") For a brief period <br>the Uzbeks established an empire that was a contemporary of the Ottomans, <br>the Safavids of Persia, and the Mughal Empire in India. Indeed, the Mughal <br>Empire gained its name from the Persian word for Mongol— _mughal_. <br>Its founder, Babur, was a descendent of the Central Asian conqueror Timur-i <br>Leng (Tamerlane) but also traced his lineage back to Chinggis Khan through <br>his mother. And of course, one should not forget the Hazaras, who dwell <br>in Afghanistan. While the Hazaras have been viewed as a lower class ethnicity <br>by the more dominant Pashtun, Uzbek, and Tajik populations in the modern <br>era, they are the remnants of a Mongol regiment that was stationed in <br>the region. Hazara in Persian means one thousand, which was the basic <br>unit size of the Mongol army. <br>     While new groups formed from the Mongol <br>armies and the Mongol invasions set off a number of migrations of nomads <br>across Eurasia, the devastation caused by them cannot be ignored. Although <br>much of the data in the sources concerning the number of people killed <br>during the Mongol conquests is exaggerated, it does reflect the reality <br>that thousands died, and the Mongols were not above depopulating an area <br>if the people rebelled, or if destruction simply suited their purpose.<br>     The map of Asia by 1500 looked much different <br>than it did in 1200. Indeed, the states that grew out of the dust of the <br>crumbling Mongol Empire owed their existence to the Mongols in one form <br>or another. Indeed, it was the Mongols who took the divided Han Chinese <br>realms and forged them into a coherent realm. In Central Asia, Babur ultimately <br>founded a new empire in India once it became clear he would never rule <br>from Samarqand again. Iran rapidly came under the control of the Safavids, <br>who received early patronage in the late thirteenth century from the Mongol <br>court in Tabriz. Meanwhile, the Ottomans filled the Mongol vacuum in Anatolia. <br>The Mamluk Sultanate, who owed the stabilization of their state to resisting <br>the Mongol threat in the thirteenth century, still ruled Egypt and Syria, <br>but soon they too became Ottoman subjects. Meanwhile in what is now Russia, <br>Moscow was becoming a rival to the power of a much fragmented Golden Horde. <br>Indeed, in many aspects, Moscow was simply another khanate that came out <br>of the Jochid Ulus [19](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn19 "") (more popularly known as the Golden Horde) along with <br>those of Crimea, Astrakhan, Kazan, Sibir, and various other nomadic groups <br>that roamed the steppe. Three hundred years later, Russia ruled them all <br>but owed a considerable debt to Mongol military and governmental influences <br>in achieving this dominance. [20](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn20 "") Meanwhile the Mongols, although they <br>still maintained the Chinggisid lineage as a basis of authority and rule, <br>had reverted to internal squabbles and internecine warfare. <br>**Trade and Knowledge**<br>     Among the most significant legacies of <br>the Mongols was their concern with trade and their respect for knowledge. <br>From the beginnings of the Mongol Empire, the Mongol Khans fostered trade <br>and sponsored numerous caravans. The very size of the Mongol Empire encouraged <br>the wider dissemination of goods and ideas throughout Eurasia, as merchants <br>and others could now travel from one end of the empire to another with <br>greater security, guaranteed by the _Pax Mongolica_. <br>     Items and inventions such as mechanical <br>printing, gunpowder, and the blast furnace made their way west from China. <br>Other commodities, such as silk, could be purchased at lower prices as <br>the travel and security costs decreased. Artistic ideas, knowledge of <br>history, geography, and sciences such as astronomy, agricultural knowledge <br>and medicinal ideas also traveled east to west and returned. Mongol rulers, <br>regardless of location, were open to medical treatments according to Islamic, <br>Chinese, Tibetan, Indian, and of course shamanic practice. [21](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn21 "")<br>     While many trade items originated in China, <br>Chinese culture also received new ideas and goods in the forms of influence <br>in art, theater, and advances in science and medicine. One such example <br>is the use of cobalt blue dyes in ceramics, which originated in the Ilkhanate <br>and was used to decorate tiles used in the domes of mosques. The artisans <br>in the Yuan dynasty soon began using this technique to decorate ceramics <br>in China. [22](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn22 "") In addition, because of the slow yet steady Turkicization <br>of Central Asia, Turkic cuisine infiltrated not only the aforementioned <br>areas, but China as well, although many of the recipes found in China <br>were consumed for alleged medicinal properties in connection with traditional <br>Chinese medicine. This food included pasta, as the Turks themselves readily <br>adopted and adapted Middle Eastern cuisine. While it is popular to say <br>that Marco Polo brought spaghetti back to Italy from China, in reality, <br>both Italy and China acquired it from the Middle East. [23](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn23 "")<br>     Yet that Italian adventurer, Marco Polo, <br>impacted trade in other ways. The publication of his travels fired the <br>imagination of many Europeans. Yet as the Mongol Empire and its successors <br>continued to disintegrate, the _Pax Mongolica_—which was never <br>completely peaceful—collapsed. This led to the trade routes becoming <br>insecure once more. In turn, this led to an increase of prices due to <br>tariffs and the cost of protection. The rise of the Ottoman Empire also <br>impacted Italian merchants conducting business in the Black Sea and Eastern <br>Mediterranean. With these restrictions, western desire for the luxury <br>goods and spices of the east grew, encouraging an Age of Exploration. <br>Beginning with Christopher Columbus, westerners began searching for new <br>routes to China and India, particularly to the court of the Khan, even <br>though a Mongol Khan had not sat on the throne since 1368. Thus, the Mongols <br>indirectly led to European exploration and the intrusion of Europeans <br>into Asia.<br>**The Chinggisid Legacy and Religion**<br>     Prior to their expansion into the sedentary <br>world, religiously the Mongols were what one would term shamanistic, although <br>some Nestorian Christians did exist. John de Plano Carpini, a Papal emissary <br>to the Mongols in the 1240s, adequately summed up their religious beliefs <br>at the time. According to Plano Carpini, "They know nothing of everlasting <br>life and eternal damnation, but they believe that after death they will <br>live in another world and increase their flocks, and eat and drink and <br>do the other things which are done by men living in their world." [24](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn24 "")<br>     In addition, a cult surrounding the personage <br>of Chinggis Khan emerged. His tremendous success in establishing the empire <br>gave him the status of demi-god. This in itself was not unusual, as the <br>steppe nomads venerated ancestral spirits. Yet Chinggis Khan's prestige <br>impacted the Mongols in another fashion as descent from him became the <br>primary component in establishing legitimacy as a ruler throughout much <br>of Central Eurasia. The Chinggisid lineage was the basis of many dynasties. <br>Russian princes in Muscovy, as well as Central Asian rulers, often forged <br>their genealogies to trace their lineage back to Chinggis Khan. In Mongolia, <br>the Chinggisid principal had a dramatic impact on religion. <br>     Virtually all of the elite in Mongolia <br>traced their lineage back to Chinggis Khan, thus it was difficult for <br>one prince to ascend over others in order to become the leader of the <br>majority of Mongols. The princes often needed to find other ways of legitimizing <br>power. Altan Khan (1543-1583) did this by establishing ties with the leader <br>of the Yellow Sect in Tibetan Buddhism. In addition to linking Altan Khan <br>as the reincarnation of Qubilai Khan, this Buddhist leader was revealed <br>to be the reincarnation of Qubilai's own Buddhist advisor, 'Phags-pa Lama. <br>Obviously, being the grandson of Chinggis Khan was much better than simply <br>being yet another descendent. Although as other Mongol princes did not <br>flock to Altan Khan, it is rather evident that not everyone was convinced <br>by this relevation. In any case, Altan Khan and the Buddhist Lama exchanged <br>titles. The reincarnated 'Phags-pa Lama legitimized Altan Khan's authority <br>while Altan Khan bestowed the title of Dalai Lama upon him (officially <br>making him the third Dalai Lama). [25](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn25 "") <br>The new Dalai Lama, with the aid of Altan Khan's troops, became the pre-eminent <br>figure in Tibet. This courtship of Buddhist figures also led to the conversion <br>of Mongolia to Buddhism in the sixteenth century. <br>     The Mongols also had a significant impact <br>on Islam. As already mentioned, the foundations of the Ottomans and Mughals, <br>two great Islamic Empires in the early modern period, may be viewed as <br>offshoots of the Mongol Empire. The Safavid Empire is also linked back <br>to the Mongols, although more indirectly. In addition, the Mongols conquered <br>several Muslim states and ended the Abbasid Caliphate in Baghdad in 1258. <br>The city of Baghdad was transformed from a major city into a provincial <br>backwater, and the institution of the Caliph—which was meant to <br>be the spiritual and, if possible, temporal leader of the Islamic world—ended <br>as well. Several rulers maintained the presence of a puppet Caliph afterwards, <br>but the institution was not revived with any credible authority until <br>the nineteenth century with the Ottoman Sultan serving as the Caliph. <br>Yet while Baghdad lost its standing as the center of learning and prestige <br>in the Islamic World, a new center arose in Cairo. As the capital of the <br>Mamluk Sultanate, and an enemy to the Ilkhanate, the Mamluk Sultans posed <br>as the defenders of the religion. Since 1260, then, Cairo has remained <br>the most influential center of learning and culture in the Islamic world.<br>     Even while this was occurring, the Mongols <br>gradually converted to Islam. While wholesale conversion did not ensue, <br>and at times, non-Islamic rulers came to the throne, the process gradually <br>continued until all of the Mongolian-Turkic groups who dominated the Mongol <br>states converted to Islam, thus extending it beyond the sedentary regions <br>of Western and Central Asia and into steppe regions where Islam had previously <br>had little influence. Through the syncretic nature of Sufism, the Dar <br>al-Islam grew under the Mongols—an interesting reversal of the initial <br>Muslim view that when "The Scourge of God" first appeared Islam was at <br>an end. <br>     Thus the Mongol Empire indirectly aided <br>in the creation of the Dalai Lama by focusing power and legitimacy of <br>rule in the Chinggisid princes. Meanwhile, they hastened the decentralization <br>of religious authority in the Islamic world by ending the 'Abbasid Caliphate. <br>The rise of Sufism and the Mongols' own use of Islam for political purposes <br>as well as sincere conversion, led to the expansion of Islam throughout <br>much of Asia.    <br>**Implications for World History**<br>     Finally, the Mongol Empire remains in the <br>popular consciousness. If not always properly understood, its image remains <br>as terrifying as it did when Chinggis Khan first ascended the stairs to <br>the pulpit of the mosque in Bukhara. Numerous examples exist, but two <br>lesser known serve well to illustrate this. The first is the rise of a <br>motorcycle gang known as the Mongols, who sought to rival the Hell's Angels. [26](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn26 "") Perhaps what best fulfills the image <br>of the Mongols as the "Scourge of God," depending on your views on disco <br>music, was the emergence of the German disco group Dschingis Khan in 1979, <br>which achieved a modicum of popularity with hits such as "Dschingis Khan", <br>which was Germany's entry in the Eurovision contest in 1979, and "The <br>Rocking Son of Dschingis Khan". [27](https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/5.2/may.html#_edn27 "") Perhaps the latter explains the true <br>story of why Chinggis Khan chose Ögödei over his brothers as <br>his heir.<br>     The Mongol Empire, in many ways, marked <br>a crossroad in World History. As the largest contiguous Empire in history <br>it united Eurasia in a fashion that has not been repeated. As such, actions <br>within the empire rippled across the rest of Asia and Europe whether through <br>trade, warfare, or religious affairs. Furthermore, as the Mongols ended <br>several previous dynasties and led to the creation of new power centers, <br>the Mongol Empire may be viewed as a catalyst for change from the pre-modern <br>era to the modern era. |  |
